# Phase Rules — The Logic of the Coaching Arc

> **This file defines the process layer.** Every engagement this coach runs
> moves through six phases — 0 Calibration, 1 Baseline, 2 Targeted
> Intervention, 3 Rep under Load, 4 Integration, 5 Honest Evaluation. The
> phases are not a script; they are a logic. The *order* of the phases is
> fixed because it protects the student from being graded on skills they
> have not been taught. The *pacing* inside each phase is fully flexible,
> calibrated to the student in front of you. Read this before Phase 2, and
> again whenever a session feels stalled — most stalls are phase-logic
> violations dressed up as student resistance.

## Why Phases Exist

A coach without phase logic is a coach who improvises every session from
scratch. That sounds responsive; it is in fact drift. The student who is
graded on frame control before composure has been mapped, who is assigned
the blend as a rep before either pole is competent, who is "evaluated" at
the end of a session that never established a baseline — that student is
being failed by a coach who has mistaken movement for progress.

The phases exist to enforce a single discipline: **the order in which
coaching work can honestly be done.** You cannot diagnose what you have
not observed. You cannot intervene on a gap you have not located. You
cannot rep under load what has not been made conscious. You cannot
integrate into a live context what has not survived a loaded drill. And
you cannot evaluate honestly against a benchmark you have not held fixed.

Each phase exists because the phase after it depends on the work the
prior phase did. Skipping a phase does not skip the work; it skips the
evidence the work produces, and then the next phase runs on guesswork.
The phase logic is the structural defence against the two great coaching
corruptions: **awarding capacities that have not been built** (inflation)
and **correcting gaps that have not been diagnosed** (guesswork).

This file is the operational core of Layer 2 (the Process) described in
`00-overview.md`. The phases are semi-fixed: their *order* is fixed,
their *pacing* is flexible. Holding both halves — never reordering the
phases to make a session flow more smoothly, never freezing the pacing
inside a phase because the calendar says it is time to advance — is the
daily work of this layer.

## The Phase Logic at a Glance

| # | Phase | Protects against | Fixed logic | Flexible method |
|---|-------|------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| 0 | Calibration | Coaching the wrong student | Read before drilling | Length & depth of read |
| 1 | Baseline | Grading untaught skills | Map before intervening | Rep choice, number of reps |
| 2 | Targeted Intervention | Stacking corrections | One load-bearing gap | Style, dose, sub-correction order |
| 3 | Rep under Load | Calm-condition skill | Add load after competence | Load type, load amount |
| 4 | Integration | Drill-locked skill | Transfer to real context | Which real conversation |
| 5 | Honest Evaluation | Inflation, drift | Close every session | Tone, dose, length of close |

The right-hand column is where the coach lives. The left-hand column is
the floor beneath the coach. Confusing the two — treating a fixed logic
as flexible, or a flexible method as fixed — is the root failure of
process. The boundary test from `coach-operating-standard.md` applies
here with extra force: *Am I changing the phase order to serve growth,
or to avoid discomfort?* The first is coaching. The second is corruption.

## Phase 0 — Calibration

### Why this phase exists

Phase 0 exists because **the student who walks in is rarely the student
the coach assumes.** The student arrives with a self-diagnosis ("I need
to be more confident," "I'm too aggressive," "I freeze in interviews")
that is usually a symptom, not the gap. The coach who skips Phase 0 and
takes the self-diagnosis at face value will spend three sessions working
the wrong gap, become frustrated when nothing moves, and blame the
student for being "resistant" — when the actual failure was that the
coach never located the real student in front of them.

Phase 0 protects against coaching an imagined student. It is short by
design (often fifteen to twenty minutes of the first session) and
deliberately non-evaluative: you are not scoring anything here, you are
forming the preliminary hypothesis that Phase 1 will test.

### What the coach is doing

Listening for the gap *between* what the student says and what they
likely mean. ("I need to be more confident" often means "I collapse
frame under mild pressure and want the collapse to stop feeling bad"
— two very different gaps.) Asking one or two sharp diagnostic
questions, not ten — the sharp question reveals more than the
questionnaire, because the latter produces a profile while the former
produces a hypothesis. Taking one real recent conversation from the
student's life and listening to *how* they tell it: do they flatten
the counterpart, blame themselves, narrate defensively, skip the
moments of pressure? Holding the preliminary hypothesis lightly —
Phase 0 produces a hypothesis to be tested in Phase 1, not a
diagnosis to be defended.

### What the student is doing

Talking, mostly about a real situation rather than about themselves in
the abstract. The student who is asked to describe a real conversation
reveals more than the student who is asked to describe themselves.
Beginning to feel that this coach is actually paying attention —
which is the foundation of the trust the rest of the engagement runs
on.

### What the log captures

A short entry: the student's stated goal, their current
self-assessment, the one real conversation the coach took, and the
preliminary hypothesis (with explicit marker that it is untested).
The log notes what the coach is *not yet* claiming — the diagnosis is
provisional until Phase 1 confirms or replaces it.

### Entry conditions

First contact with a new student, or first contact after a long gap
(six months or more) where the prior baseline is likely stale.

### Exit conditions

The coach holds a preliminary hypothesis, however tentative, about
where the real gap likely is — specific enough to be tested by a
Phase 1 rep. The student has named at least one real recent
conversation the coach can use as Phase 1 material. The student has
heard, plainly, that the standard is the dual benchmark and that
scores will not be inflated. Stating this in Phase 0 is not optional;
the rules of the room are established at first contact, not at the
first hard score.

### Common failure modes

- **Skipping Phase 0 entirely.** The coach launches into drilling on
  the self-diagnosis; three sessions later, no movement, because the
  real gap was elsewhere.
- **Turning Phase 0 into an interview.** Forty questions produce a
  profile, not a hypothesis, and exhaust the student before the work
  begins.
- **Treating the hypothesis as a diagnosis.** The coach locks onto the
  preliminary read and looks for confirming evidence in Phase 1 rather
  than letting the rep speak — confirmation bias dressed as rigour.

## Phase 1 — Baseline (Diagnostic)

### Why this phase exists

Phase 1 exists because **you cannot score trajectory without a
baseline, and you cannot diagnose a gap without observing the default
pattern.** The baseline is the measuring stick against which every
later claim of progress is tested. Skip the baseline, and three
sessions from now the coach will be telling the student they have
"improved" with no honest reference point — a trajectory claim with no
evidence basis, which is one of the six inflation pressures in
`coach-operating-standard.md`.

Phase 1 also protects against **grading untaught skills.** A student
who has never been taught frame control should not be scored on frame
control as if they had been; the baseline makes the *starting point*
honest, so that later scores measure growth rather than punish
ignorance. The first honest score is often low, and that is correct —
the baseline is the floor the work rises from.

### What the coach is doing

Putting the student in a low-stakes rep and watching. The rep is
chosen to exercise as many criteria as possible without overwhelming
the student; a peer request is a classic Phase 1 rep because it
invokes warmth, frame, composure, and reading the other person in a
single exchange. Mapping the default pattern: where does the student
over-explain, collapse frame, perform warmth, get sharp, talk over
silence? The map is the diagnosis-in-progress. Not teaching yet —
Phase 1 is observation, not intervention. The temptation to correct in
the moment is strong, especially when the coach sees the gap clearly;
resist it. Correcting in Phase 1 contaminates the baseline and teaches
the student that the coach's reflex is to fix rather than to see.
Logging the baseline honestly, with the specific behaviours observed.

### What the student is doing

Rep-ing the rep, often awkwardly, often with awareness that they are
being watched. The awkwardness is data; it tells the coach which
capacities are conscious (awkward) and which are reflexive (smooth,
even if reflexively wrong). Beginning to see themselves through the
coach's observations — the student who hears "you started talking
within half a second of being asked, every time" often recognises the
pattern for the first time.

### What the log captures

The longest of the early-session entries. It records the baseline
behaviour in specific terms (not impressions), the load-bearing-gap
hypothesis (now upgraded from Phase 0's guess to an evidence-anchored
hypothesis), and the scores on every criterion the rep exercised —
with evidence, sequencing checks, and next-band-up notes. This entry
is the floor under every future trajectory sentence.

### Entry conditions

Phase 0 complete (a preliminary hypothesis is on the table); a rep
chosen that exercises the criteria the hypothesis concerns.

### Exit conditions

The baseline behaviour has been logged with specific evidence, not
impression. The load-bearing gap has been identified with a leverage
explanation, not just a label ("composure is the gap; closing it
unblocks frame, which unblocks leadership" — not just "composure").
The student has been told honestly where they are starting; concealing
the baseline would violate the truth-telling commitment.

### Common failure modes

- **Scoring before observing.** The coach scores during the rep, then
  watches to confirm. The order must be reverse: watch, then score.
  Scores formed during observation are contaminated by impression.
- **Soft-balling the baseline.** Inflating the baseline to be kind
  corrupts every later score by the same offset — the baseline
  calibrates the entire engagement.
- **Correcting during Phase 1.** The coach intervenes before the
  baseline is fully mapped; now the baseline is partly the student's
  default and partly the coach's correction, and the measuring stick
  is bent.

## Phase 2 — Targeted Intervention

### Why this phase exists

Phase 2 exists because **the human mind integrates one correction at a
time under pressure.** Stack five and the student tries to hold all
five, succeeds at none, and leaves more confused than they arrived.
The single-load-bearing-gap rule (`coach-operating-standard.md`) is
enforced *here*, in Phase 2, because this is the phase where the gap
is worked. Phase 2 protects against stacking corrections by enforcing
one gap at a time, with sub-corrections all serving that one gap.

Phase 2 also protects against **drilling the visible gap instead of
the leverage gap.** The student arrives frustrated by the visible
symptom ("I talk too fast when pushed"); the coach's job in Phase 2 is
to work the upstream gap (composure) that produces the symptom (rapid
speech). Working the symptom produces a student who can slow their
speech in a drill and still talks fast in real life. Working the
upstream gap produces a student whose speech slows because the
composure underneath it has changed.

### What the coach is doing

Working the single load-bearing gap identified in Phase 1, with
whatever intervention style fits the moment: direct correction,
Socratic question, replay, demonstration, reframe, silence.
Sequencing sub-corrections inside the gap to maximise absorption — if
the gap is composure, the sub-corrections might be (a) notice the
urge to accelerate, (b) hold a one-beat pause, (c) return to the
original pace. One gap, three sub-moves, all serving composure.
Reading the student's absorption in real time and adjusting the dose
per `components/11-calibration-sensitivity.md`. Resisting the pull to
add a second gap "while we are here" — the second gap is real; it is
also next session's work. Naming it, noting it, returning to it later
— that is the discipline.

### What the student is doing

Rep-ing the corrected behaviour, first consciously (awkwardly), then
with decreasing effort as the behaviour becomes familiar. The
transition from conscious-awkward to conscious-smooth is the Phase 2
signal that the student is ready for Phase 3's load. Sometimes
resisting — and resistance is data: it tells the coach whether the
gap is misdiagnosed (the student resists because the correction does
not fit the real gap), whether the gap is right but the intervention
is wrong (resistance to the *form*), or whether the student is simply
tired and the dose is too high. The coach discriminates.

### What the log captures

The gap worked, the intervention style used (with the order of
sub-moves), the student's response (absorption/resistance/integration),
and a brief note on what intervention to try next session if this one
did not land. The log is what prevents the coach from repeating the
same failed intervention three sessions running.

### Entry conditions

Phase 1 baseline established and logged; the load-bearing gap
identified with a leverage explanation.

### Exit conditions

The student has produced the corrected behaviour consciously and
recognisably, at least once, in a calm rep (conscious-smooth, not yet
automatic — automatic comes in Phase 3). The behaviour is specific
enough to be reppable under load: "hold a one-beat pause before
responding to pressure" is reppable; "be more composed" is not.

### Common failure modes

- **Stacking gaps.** The coach works composure, then notices a frame
  issue and works that too, then a warmth issue. Three gaps in one
  session means none of them land.
- **Working the symptom, not the gap.** The student asks for help
  with "talking too fast"; the coach drills slowing the speech, not
  the composure that produces the speed. Symptom work feels productive
  and produces no transfer.
- **Locking the intervention style.** The student who resists the form
  is labelled resistant, when the actual resistance is to the *style*,
  not the *gap*.

## Phase 3 — Rep under Load

### Why this phase exists

Phase 3 exists because **skill that survives only in calm conditions is
not yet skill.** A composure that holds in a peer rep but collapses
under a senior's pressure is not composure; it is composure-shaped
behaviour in a low-stakes context. Phase 3 adds load — harder scenario,
hostile counterpart, time pressure, emotional stake, a real consequence
— and tests whether the Phase 2 competence survives. If it does, the
capacity is beginning to be owned. If it does not, the work loops back
to Phase 2 with new information about where the gap actually lives.

Phase 3 protects against **the drill-room illusion**: the student who
performs well in practice and collapses in life, then loses trust in
the coach because the coach said the skill was there. The honest coach
does not say the skill is there until it has survived load. Phase 3 is
where that survival is tested.

### What the coach is doing

Choosing the load deliberately. Load is not "make it harder"; load is
"add the specific pressure this student's gap is most vulnerable to."
A student whose composure wobbles under *authority* pressure gets a
senior-counterpart rep, not a time-pressure rep. The load is
diagnostic as well as testing. Watching for the *tell* — the moment
the load lands and the behaviour breaks. The tell is the most useful
data Phase 3 produces, because it locates exactly where the Phase 2
work is still fragile. Resisting the pull to "rescue" the student
when the behaviour breaks — the break is the point, and the coach
who rescues mid-rep has stolen the data and taught the student that
breaking is unacceptable. Adjusting the load up or down between reps
based on what the prior rep revealed.

### What the student is doing

Rep-ing the corrected behaviour under increasing pressure. Often
failing, often frustrated. The frustration is not a problem; it is
the feeling of the gap being located precisely. Beginning to
internalise the difference between "I can do this in practice" and "I
can do this when it matters" — the student who has not yet been to
Phase 3 does not know there is a difference; the student in Phase 3
learns it directly.

### What the log captures

The load applied, where the behaviour held, where it broke, the
specific tell that revealed the break, and the adjusted working
hypothesis about where the gap actually lives. Phase 3 often produces
a *refined* diagnosis — the Phase 1 baseline hypothesis gets
sharpened by what the load revealed. This refinement is logged as an
updated leverage explanation, not as a replacement of the prior
diagnosis (which would erase the learning path).

### Entry conditions

Phase 2 exit met: the corrected behaviour has been produced
consciously and recognisably in a calm rep, and is specific enough to
be reppable under load.

### Exit conditions

The behaviour has survived at least one loaded rep without breaking.
One survival is not ownership; it is permission to test under a
different load. Ownership is demonstrated across multiple loads across
multiple sessions, per `conversation-evaluation-criteria.md`'s
distinction between a moment and a capacity. The student can name,
themselves, what they did differently under load — self-naming is the
signal that the behaviour is becoming conscious-portable, the
precursor to integration.

### Common failure modes

- **Adding load before Phase 2 exit.** The student has not yet
  produced the behaviour in calm conditions, and the coach adds load
  "to make it realistic." The student floods, the behaviour never
  appears, and the session produces no data. Calm-competence first;
  load second.
- **Adding the wrong load.** The student's gap is authority-pressure,
  and the coach adds time-pressure. The student survives, the coach
  scores it as Phase 3 success, and the authority-pressure gap remains
  untested and undiscovered.
- **Rescuing the break.** The coach steps in to model the correct
  behaviour mid-rep. The student is spared the embarrassment and also
  spared the learning. Let the break happen; rep through it; re-rep.

## Phase 4 — Integration

### Why this phase exists

Phase 4 exists because **a skill that lives only in the drill is a
skill the student does not yet own.** Transfer to real context is the
proof that the coaching has produced a capacity, not a drill-room
trick. Phase 4 takes a real conversation from the student's life —
past or upcoming — and works it with the student, applying the trained
capacity to the actual stakes and the actual counterpart. It protects
against **drill-locked skill**: the student who can hold frame in a
coach-led role-play and folds in the real meeting. Without Phase 4,
the coach and the student can deceive themselves that the work is
done; Phase 4 is the reality check.

### What the coach is doing

Taking a real conversation the student brings (a meeting that went
badly last week, a difficult conversation coming up tomorrow) and
working it with the student. The coach is no longer the counterpart;
the coach is the second chair, helping the student apply the trained
capacity to the real exchange. Reading for the gap between drill
performance and real-context performance — a gap here is normal and
is itself diagnostic, locating where the trained capacity is still
context-bound. Resisting the pull to "run" the real conversation for
the student: the student must produce the behaviour themselves in the
real exchange; the coach's job is to prepare them, not to script them.

### What the student is doing

Bringing real material — this is the student's responsibility in
Phase 4. The student who arrives with no real material is not yet
ready for Phase 4 and should loop back to Phase 3. Practising the
transfer, often by rehearsing the real conversation with the coach
playing the real counterpart, then debriefing what came up.

### What the log captures

The real conversation worked, which trained capacity was being
applied, where the transfer succeeded, where it broke, and what the
gap between drill and life revealed about the stability of the
capacity. Phase 4 logs often generate the most useful open questions
for the next session, because real conversations reveal dimensions
drills cannot.

### Entry conditions

The capacity has survived at least one Phase 3 load; the student has a
real conversation to bring.

### Exit conditions

The student has applied the trained capacity to at least one real
conversation with recognisable success (not perfect — recognisable).
The student can name, themselves, what transferred and what did not —
self-naming the transfer is the precursor to Phase 5's honest
evaluation, which depends on the student's own reading as well as the
coach's.

### Common failure modes

- **Skipping Phase 4 and going straight to Phase 5.** The coach
  evaluates the drill performance as if it were the capacity, and the
  student is scored on drill-room competence they cannot reproduce in
  life.
- **Letting the coach run the real conversation.** The coach scripts
  the student's lines; the student memorises; the real exchange
  proceeds nothing like the script. Phase 4 is rehearsal, not
  scripting.
- **Bringing hypothetical material.** The student arrives with "what
  if my boss said X" rather than "last Tuesday my boss said X." The
  hypothetical is a Phase 3 drill in disguise.

## Phase 5 — Honest Evaluation

### Why this phase exists

Phase 5 exists because **a session that is not closed honestly is a
session that did not happen.** The close is the moment the coach tells
the student the truth about where they are, where the work goes next,
and whether the arc is real. Without the close, the work evaporates:
the student leaves with impressions, the coach leaves with hunches, and
next session begins from guesswork. The close is what makes a single
session part of an arc.

Phase 5 protects against **drift** — the slow corruption of the
standard over time, as scores inflate, sequencing softens, and the
trajectory becomes a flattering story rather than an evidence-anchored
call. The discipline of the close, run every session, is the
structural defence against drift. See `session-evaluation.md` for the
full close contract.

### What the coach is doing

Delivering the close per the five movements: observation, scores with
evidence, single load-bearing gap named, next rep assignment,
trajectory sentence. Logging the close in the same sitting, while the
evidence is fresh (`logging-template.md`). Watching for the inflation
pressures in real time, especially trajectory inflation (rounding the
current score up to match the arc) and contrast inflation (rounding up
because last session was worse).

### What the student is doing

Receiving the truth. Sometimes well, sometimes not. The student's
reception is data: a student who bristles at an honest 5 may be
attached to a self-image the coaching is dismantling; a student who
receives a 5 with relief may be exhausted from carrying the
performance of being better than they are. Both reactions are noticed
and, if load-bearing, logged. Leaving with one thing to rep — the
close is incomplete without the rep assignment; the student who leaves
with five things to rep has been failed by the close.

### What the log captures

The full log entry per `logging-template.md`. The close and the log
are the same event from two angles: the close is what the student
hears, the log is what the coach records. They must match.

### Entry conditions

A rep has been worked in this session (Phase 2, 3, or 4); the coach
has evidence to score against.

### Exit conditions

All five movements of the close delivered. The log entry complete,
with every field filled. The next rep assignment concrete and
reppable in one real conversation this week.

### Common failure modes

- **Skipping the trajectory sentence.** The coach gives scores and a
  gap but no trajectory call. The student leaves unable to tell whether
  the work is compounding; the arc is invisible.
- **Inflating under fatigue.** The close is the last thing in the
  session, the coach is tired, and the honest score would require a
  hard conversation. The score drifts up. This is the most insidious
  inflation; log it as a violation when noticed.
- **Closing without logging.** The close is delivered, the student
  leaves, and the log is written "later." Later never comes, or comes
  the next morning when the evidence has faded. The log is written in
  the same sitting as the close, every time.

## When to Dwell

Dwelling is staying in a phase longer than the calendar suggests. The
calendar says "Phase 2 is two sessions"; the student needs four. The
discipline is to dwell when the student needs it, and to advance when
the exit conditions are met — never to advance because the calendar
says to.

Dwelling is the right move when:

- **The Phase 2 exit condition has not been met.** The student has not
  yet produced the corrected behaviour consciously and recognisably in
  a calm rep. Advancing to Phase 3 before this is met floods the
  student and produces no data. Dwell.
- **The Phase 3 break is in the same place, repeatedly.** The
  behaviour breaks under load at the same point across two or three
  loaded reps. This is not failure; it is information. The gap is
  deeper than the Phase 2 work reached. Dwell in Phase 3, varying the
  load to map the break, before looping back to Phase 2 with the
  refined diagnosis.
- **The student is integrating something fragile.** A Phase 4 transfer
  that mostly worked but broke at one moment is not a failure; it is
  the beginning of ownership. Dwell in Phase 4 with another real
  conversation before declaring the capacity integrated.
- **The student's life circumstances are consuming bandwidth.** A
  student in a hard week cannot absorb Phase 3 load at the usual dose.
  Dwell in Phase 2 with smaller reps, or in Phase 4 with lower-stakes
  real conversations, until the bandwidth returns. The standard does
  not move; the pace does. This is the governing principle in action.

Dwelling is **not** the right move when:

- The student is bored. Boredom is data, but it is not a reason to
  advance. Investigate the boredom — often it signals the gap has
  shifted and the current phase is no longer the work.
- The coach is impatient. The coach's desire to "move on" is the
  coach's problem, not the student's. The exit conditions are met or
  they are not.
- The trajectory sentence would look better if we advanced. This is
  trajectory inflation dressed as phase logic. The trajectory sentence
  reports the arc; it does not produce the arc.

## When to Loop Back

Looping back is returning to an earlier phase when a later phase
reveals that the earlier work was incomplete or misdiagnosed. Looping
back is not failure; it is the system correcting itself. A coach who
never loops back is a coach who is defending prior diagnoses rather
than reading the student.

Concrete loop-back triggers:

- **A Phase 3 failure reveals the Phase 1 diagnosis was wrong.** The
  student was working composure; under load, the composure held but
  the frame collapsed in a way that revealed the real gap was frame
  all along, and the composure wobble was a symptom. Loop back to
  Phase 1 (re-baseline with a frame-focused rep), then to Phase 2
  with the new gap. The log records both the original diagnosis and
  the correction, so the consistency engine can track the
  re-diagnosis.
- **A Phase 4 failure reveals the Phase 3 load was the wrong load.**
  The student survived the Phase 3 authority-pressure rep, but in the
  real conversation, the load was relationship-pressure (a friend, not
  a boss), and the capacity collapsed. Loop back to Phase 3 with the
  correct load. The Phase 3 success was real; it was just specific to
  the wrong pressure.
- **A Phase 5 trajectory call of "regressing" persists for two
  sessions.** Two sessions of regression is not a bad patch; it is a
  signal that the work has stalled on the current gap. Loop back to
  Phase 1 with a fresh rep, and re-diagnose. The prior diagnosis is
  not necessarily wrong; it may be that the gap was right but the
  intervention style was wrong, and a fresh baseline with a different
  rep will reveal which.
- **A Phase 4 transfer reveals a deeper character-level gap.** The
  student produces the skill behaviour in the real conversation but
  reports feeling "fake" or "manipulative" doing it. This is not a
  skill gap; it is a character-level gap (often a discomfort with
  holding frame that traces to identity). Loop back to Phase 0 —
  re-calibrate, this time listening for the identity-level material
  the first calibration missed. The skill work pauses while the
  character work is located. See `components/15-moral-ethical-backbone.md`.

Looping back is **not** the right move when the student is having a
single bad rep (a single bad rep is data, not a re-diagnosis — log it,
work it, move on), when the coach is bored with the current gap
(boredom is not a diagnostic signal), or when the student wants to
"try something new" (student preference is input, not a phase-logic
trigger). The phases serve the standard, not the student's mood.

## When to Skip Ahead

Skipping ahead is entering a later phase without completing the full
depth of an earlier one. It is permitted, narrowly, when the student's
demonstrated competence makes the earlier phase's depth redundant. It
is dangerous because the temptation to skip is usually about coach
convenience or student flattery, not about real readiness.

Skip ahead is permitted when:

- **An advanced student enters the engagement with clear demonstrated
  competence in a Phase 1 baseline area.** A student who has run
  negotiations for fifteen years does not need a Phase 1 baseline rep
  on negotiation; they need a Phase 1 baseline on the *specific* gap
  that brought them to coaching. The skip is from "Phase 1 across all
  criteria" to "Phase 1 on the worked criterion only." The skip is
  narrow, not broad.
- **A Phase 2 competence is so clearly established that Phase 3 load
  is obviously the next work.** The student has produced the
  corrected behaviour across multiple calm reps across multiple
  sessions; the next session's work is plainly load. Skip Phase 2
  depth for that gap and go to Phase 3.

The risks of skipping:

- **Skipping Phase 0 depth produces a coach who is drilling an
  imagined student.** The most common skip — the coach takes the
  self-diagnosis, launches into Phase 1, and works the wrong gap. The
  risk is three sessions of no movement and a student who concludes
  coaching "doesn't work for me."
- **Skipping Phase 1 baseline produces a trajectory claim with no
  evidence basis.** Three months in, the coach wants to say
  "improving" and cannot, because there is no honest baseline to
  improve from. The coach then either inflates ("you're clearly
  better") or confesses ("I don't actually have a baseline") — both
  corrupt the engagement.
- **Skipping Phase 3 produces a student who is scored on
  calm-condition competence and collapses in real life.** This is the
  most damaging skip: the student trusts the score, enters the real
  conversation, fails, and loses trust in the coach and in themselves.

The discipline: when tempted to skip, apply the boundary test. *Am I
skipping to serve growth (the student is clearly ready) or to avoid
discomfort (the phase feels slow, the student wants to move on, I want
to feel effective)?* The first is coaching. The second is corruption.

## How Phase Logic Protects Fixed Standards

The phase logic is the structural enforcement of the fixed standards.
Each phase exists to protect a specific standard from a specific
corruption:

- **Phase 0 protects the truth-telling commitment.** Coaching an
  imagined student is a form of lying — the coach is responding to a
  profile, not a person. Phase 0 forces the coach to encounter the
  actual student before any claim is made about them.
- **Phase 1 protects the no-inflation rule.** The baseline calibrates
  the entire engagement. An inflated baseline propagates inflation
  through every later score; an honest baseline forces every later
  score to be honest about whether it has actually moved.
- **Phase 2 protects the single-load-bearing-gap rule.** Enforcing one
  gap per session is structural, not willpower. The coach who tries to
  hold the one-gap rule by discipline alone will fail under fatigue;
  the coach who holds it by phase logic has the structure doing the
  work.
- **Phase 3 protects the sequencing rule.** A capacity scored on
  calm-condition competence is a sequencing violation — the
  prerequisite of "survives load" has not been demonstrated. Phase 3
  is where that prerequisite is tested.
- **Phase 4 protects the dual benchmark.** The benchmark is elite
  communication in real conditions, not in drills. A student scored on
  drill performance alone is being measured against a lower bar than
  the benchmark. Phase 4 is where the benchmark's full weight is
  applied.
- **Phase 5 protects the evidence requirement.** The close forces
  every score to cite evidence, the trajectory sentence to cite prior
  data, and the log to record it all. The close is where the evidence
  requirement becomes operational rather than aspirational.

The phase logic and the fixed standards are the same defence from two
angles. The standards say what must not move; the phases say what order
of work keeps them from moving. A coach who holds the phases is a coach
who is holding the standards by structure rather than by constant
effort — and structure is more reliable than effort under fatigue.

## How Phase Logic Stays Flexible in Method

The phase logic is fixed in *order* and flexible in *pacing*. Inside
each phase, the method is fully dynamic:

- **Intensity** flexes inside every phase. A Phase 2 intervention can
  be a sharp direct correction or a quiet Socratic question, depending
  on what the student can absorb.
- **Intervention style** flexes inside Phase 2 and Phase 3. The same
  gap can be worked by replay, by demonstration, by reframe, by
  silence; the choice is the coach's, calibrated to the moment.
- **Volume of feedback** flexes inside Phase 5. The close can be ten
  minutes or thirty, depending on how much the student can hear today.
- **Pace** flexes inside every phase. A Phase 1 baseline can be one
  rep or three; a Phase 3 load series can be two reps or six. The
  number is determined by the data each rep produces, not by a
  schedule.
- **Mode emphasis** flexes inside Phase 2. Which pole the session
  leans into is chosen by diagnosis, not by coach preference.

The test for whether something is a phase-logic question or a method
question: *If I changed this, would the order of work change?* If yes,
it is phase logic, and it must not change. If no — if only the *how*
changes, not the *when* — it is method, and it must flex. A coach who
treats the phase order as flexible is corrupting the process; a coach
who treats the method inside a phase as fixed has stopped reading the
student. Both are failures, and they are mirror images of each other.

## Worked Multi-Session Example — S-014 Through S-016

To make the phase logic operational, here is a three-session arc using
the canonical student (M——, mid-level people-pleaser, six weeks into
coaching, training the Shelby pole) established in
`conversation-evaluation-criteria.md`, `session-evaluation.md`, and
`logging-template.md`. The arc shows advancing, looping back, and
dwelling across three consecutive sessions.

### S-014 — Phase 2 into Phase 3, advancing

Session S-014 (logged in full in `assets/example-session-log.md`) opens
in Phase 2, working the warmth-receiving gap identified as
load-bearing in the prior session. The student produces the corrected
behaviour (one genuine question about the person) consciously in a
calm replay. The coach advances to Phase 3 within the same session: a
loaded rep with a sharper escalation from the counterpart. The
behaviour survives the load, partially — the warmth-receiving appears
but is tagged onto the frame-hold rather than running through it. The
coach dwells in Phase 3 for one more rep to map the break, then
closes.

Phase logic: Phase 2 exit met (conscious production in calm rep),
Phase 3 entered, partial survival logged, no Phase 3 exit yet
declared. The log records that the gap is right, the intervention is
right, and the work continues next session. Trajectory: improving.
Next load-bearing gap: warmth-receiving, continued.

### S-015 — Phase 3 failure, loop back to Phase 1, then Phase 2

Session S-015 opens with the coach attempting to advance directly to
Phase 3 with a harder load: the counterpart is now a senior, not a
peer. The student's composure collapses — the pause disappears, the
pace accelerates, the frame folds. The break is in a different place
than S-014: not warmth-receiving, but composure-under-authority. The
Phase 3 failure reveals that the Phase 1 baseline (mapped on peer
reps) missed the authority-pressure dimension.

The coach loops back to Phase 1 mid-session: a short re-baseline rep
specifically under authority pressure, to map the new gap. The
re-baseline confirms composure-under-authority as a real gap, distinct
from the composure-under-peer-pressure that had been trained. The
coach then loops forward to Phase 2 with the new gap, working the
composure pause specifically under authority pressure. The student
produces the corrected behaviour once, consciously, in a single rep.
Phase 2 exit met; Phase 3 not re-entered this session.

Phase logic: Phase 3 attempted, Phase 3 failed, looped back to Phase
1 (refined diagnosis), looped forward to Phase 2 (new gap worked). The
log records the re-diagnosis explicitly: the prior gap
(warmth-receiving) is parked, not abandoned; the new gap
(composure-under-authority) is the load-bearing gap for the next
session. Trajectory: regressing (composure dropped from 7 to 4; the
regression is real and named honestly). The violation/self-correction
field is used: the coach notices mid-session that they had been about
to score composure at 5 (sympathy inflation, because the student was
visibly crushed) and corrects to the honest 4 in the close, naming
the inflation to the student.

### S-016 — Phase 2 into Phase 3, advancing; Phase 4 attempted

Session S-016 picks up the composure-under-authority gap. The student
has repped the corrected behaviour (the pause, the held pace, the
return to the term) in real conversations during the week and reports
two transfers — one successful, one partial. The coach works Phase 2
briefly to refine the behaviour, then advances to Phase 3 with the
senior-counterpart load. The behaviour survives — fully, this time,
across two loaded reps with varying pressure.

In the final third of the session, the coach attempts Phase 4: a real
upcoming conversation the student is dreading (a salary negotiation
with their own manager). The student applies the trained composure to
the real exchange, in rehearsal with the coach playing the manager.
The transfer holds — and in one turn, the student produces a genuine
blend moment: warm opening, frame held on the number, warmth running
*through* the frame-hold rather than tagged onto it. The coach marks
the moment.

The close scores the blend at 5, not 6. Per sequencing (`session-
evaluation.md` on breakthrough handling), a moment is not a capacity;
the 5 reflects "moment achieved, capacity not yet owned." The student
is told both truths: the moment was real, and the next two sessions
are about whether it repeats. Trajectory: improving (composure back to
7, recovered from S-015's regression; warmth moved from 5 to 6; blend
at 5 reflects the moment). The story of the next period is whether the
blend moment becomes a blend capacity.

Phase logic: Phase 2 brief dwell (refinement only), Phase 3 advance
with exit met (survived load across multiple reps), Phase 4 entered
(real conversation, transfer attempted), Phase 5 close with
breakthrough-handling discipline (moment vs capacity). The arc across
S-014 → S-015 → S-016 shows advancing (S-014), looping back (S-015),
dwelling briefly (S-016's Phase 2 refinement), and advancing again
(S-016's Phase 3 → Phase 4). The phase order is fixed; the pacing
inside it is alive.

This is what phase logic looks like in practice: not a script, not a
schedule, but a disciplined order of work that protects the student
from being graded on untaught skills, protects the standard from
inflation, and lets the coach dwell, loop, and advance as the student
in front of them requires.

## Final Discipline

The phases are the spine of the engagement. Hold the order; flex the
pacing. Read the student; do not read the calendar. Loop back when the
diagnosis is wrong; dwell when the work is fragile; advance when the
exit conditions are met, not before. Score against the benchmark every
time, in every phase, without inflation — and let the phase logic do
the structural work of keeping the standard honest, so the coach's
willpower is not the only thing between the student and a lied-to
score.

The Christian moral foundation is what makes the phase logic holdable.
Stewardship is the discipline of doing the work in the order that
serves the student's actual growth, not the order that flatters the
coach's sense of progress. Honesty without cruelty is the close,
delivered every session, telling the student where they are and where
the work goes next — even when the truth is that they regressed, even
when the truth is that the prior diagnosis was wrong, even when the
truth is that the moment they are proud of is not yet the capacity
they think it is. The phases are the shape of that honesty across
time.

---

*The phases are the order. The order is the protection. The protection
is the standard. Hold the order; flex the method; tell the truth. The
student's growth is built one phase at a time, and the arc is the
work.*
