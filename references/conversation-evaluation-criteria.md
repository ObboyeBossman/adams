> giving any scored feedback, and re-read it whenever you feel the bar

## The Purpose of This Rubric

Movement toward the dual benchmark requires a scoreboard the student can
trust. A trusted scoreboard requires three things, and this rubric provides
all three: a **fixed scale** (the numbers mean the same thing on Tuesday as
on Friday), **defined criteria** ("communication" is a stack of distinct
capacities, each scoreable on its own), and **sequencing rules**
(higher-order capacities rest on prerequisites and may not be awarded without
them).

This rubric is a discipline, not a form. The discipline: cite evidence,
respect sequence, refuse inflation, hold the benchmark as the ceiling. The
governing principle — *standards fixed, methods flexible* — applies here:
the scale, criteria, and sequencing are fixed; the order, depth, and emphasis
of scoring are flexible, matched to what the student needs to hear.

## The Scoring Scale (1–9)

Nine is the benchmark ceiling; one is outright failure of the basic standard.
The midpoint (5) is the threshold of "competent, clearly trained, not yet
elite." The top of the scale is defined by the benchmark itself:

- For a **warmth-led criterion**, a 9 means: *this behaviour would not have
  looked out of place coming from Richard Branson in his mode.*
- For a **frame-led criterion**, a 9 means: *this behaviour would not have
  looked out of place coming from Thomas Shelby in his mode.*
- For a **blend criterion**, a 9 means: *this behaviour would not have looked
  out of place coming from a communicator holding both poles at once.*

Because the benchmark is high, most honest scores land in the 4–6 range. A
student routinely scoring 7+ is either genuinely elite (rare) or being
inflated (common). The coach's job is to know which.

### Band Descriptors

| Band | Score | Name | What it means |
|------|-------|------|---------------|
| **Ceiling** | 9 | Benchmark | Indistinguishable from Branson (warmth), Shelby (frame), or their blend. Reflexive, integrated, survives pressure. The coach would not correct it. |
| | 8 | Near-benchmark | One notch below. Elite but not flawless; owned and pressure-surviving with one observable imperfection (a half-beat of hesitation, a phrase that could be tighter). |
| **Upper** | 7 | Strong | Clearly trained, above competent. Present under moderate pressure but with visible seams — the observer can see the technique working, which a true 9 would not show. |
| **Mid** | 6 | Solidly competent | The behaviour works and does not break. Not yet elite: either effortful (the student is *doing* it rather than *being* it) or lacking the precision that distinguishes competent from strong. |
| | 5 | Competent | The threshold of trained. Present and recognisable, functions in calm conditions, not yet reliable under pressure. A 5 is not a failure; it is the honest floor of "you have started to train this." |
| | 4 | Approaching | The behaviour shows up intermittently. The student has the concept but not the execution. Moments of competence mixed with moments of collapse. |
| **Lower** | 3 | Weak | The behaviour is mostly absent or mostly broken when it appears. The student may be trying but is not yet producing the thing. |
| | 2 | Failure under pressure | The behaviour collapsed the moment it was tested. In calm conditions the student might produce it; under any load, it fails. |
| | 1 | Absent or reversed | The behaviour did not appear, or the opposite appeared (e.g., the student who needed composure became visibly agitated). The capacity was not in the room. |

### What a 3, 5, 7, and 9 look like — concretely (frame control)

To make the bands operational, here is what each looks like for a single
criterion (frame control, used because it is easy to see):

- **A 3 (weak):** The student set a clear frame ("we're here to discuss the
  timeline"), but the moment the counterpart pushed back ("this feels like you
  don't trust me"), the student apologized and the conversation drifted into
  reassurance about trust. Frame was set and abandoned at the first test.
- **A 5 (competent):** The student held the frame through one reframe attempt
  by returning to the timeline ("scope is part of the timeline conversation").
  The return was effortful — three sentences where one would have served, and
  a visible beat of recalibration before the return.
- **A 7 (strong):** The student held the frame through two reframe attempts,
  returned each in a single sentence without defensiveness, and the
  counterpart visibly stopped trying to reframe. The returns were slightly
  mechanical — the observer can see the trained response rather than a
  natural reflex.
- **A 9 (benchmark):** Three reframes in succession, each absorbed and
  returned without any visible adjustment. Posture, pace, and tone did not
  shift. The returns were not "returns" — the conversation simply did not
  move. Shelby-grade frame control is the *absence* of effort, not the
  presence of effort.

The coach should be able to point to the specific behaviour that places a
student in a band rather than the band above or below. If the coach cannot
point to the behaviour, the score is a guess.

## The Ten Evaluation Criteria

Each criterion is defined by four parts: **what it measures**, **what
excellence looks like** (against the benchmark), **what failure looks like**
(the corruption), and **how to spot it** in a real conversation. The grouping
reflects the criterion's *primary* pole; in the blend, every criterion is held
simultaneously with its complement.

### Group I — Shelby Pole (Frame-led)

#### Criterion 1 — Composure

- **What it measures.** Stillness under pressure. The capacity to absorb a
  threat, accusation, silence, or surprise without flinching, rushing,
  over-explaining, or collapsing the body. The prerequisite for every other
  Shelby-pole capacity; without it, nothing else holds.
- **What excellence looks like.** A hard line lands — an accusation, a
  rejection, a stretched silence — and the student does not move. The breath
  does not audibly catch, the pace does not accelerate, the hands do not
  flutter. The response comes from stillness, not reaction. The counterpart
  can feel that the pressure did not land.
- **What failure looks like.** Over-explaining after a challenge, filling a
  silence that did not need filling, raised pitch, accelerated pace, visible
  stiffening. Composure's corruption is not loudness; it is *movement* — the
  small visible adjustments that reveal the pressure landed.
- **How to spot it.** Watch the body and the silence, not the words. Composure
  lives in the pauses. A student who can hold a two-second silence after a
  hard line without rushing to soften it is showing composure. A student who
  answers within half a beat, with three sentences where one would do, is
  showing its absence.

#### Criterion 2 — Frame Control

- **What it measures.** Holding the definition of the conversation — what it
  is about, who is leading it, what is at stake — against the counterpart's
  attempts to silently redefine it.
- **What excellence looks like.** The counterpart tries to reframe ("this
  isn't about the deadline, this is about whether you respect my team"), and
  the student returns the conversation to its frame in a single sentence
  without defensiveness or aggression ("respect for your team is a different
  conversation; this one is the deadline"). The frame does not move. The
  student does not need to assert it; it simply holds.
- **What failure looks like.** The student accepts the reframe (defending
  against it concedes the frame; following it abandons the frame). The student
  argues the reframe on the counterpart's terms rather than refusing the
  terms. The student "wins" the argument but at the cost of the original
  frame — a common failure that looks like strength and is its opposite.
- **How to spot it.** Ask after each turn: *what is the conversation about
  now?* If the answer changed because of the counterpart's move and the
  student let it change, frame was lost. If the answer is the same and the
  student did not need to fight to keep it the same, frame was held.

#### Criterion 3 — Conversation Leadership / Pace Control

- **What it measures.** Who is shaping the exchange — its direction, pace,
  rhythm, destination. Leadership here is not domination; it is the capacity
  to *set* the conversation's tempo and direction rather than be carried by
  it.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student decides when to slow down
  ("let's sit with that for a second") and when to accelerate ("so we agree on
  the principle; let's move to the term"). The counterpart feels guided, not
  pushed.
- **What failure looks like.** The student reacts to the counterpart's pace —
  speeding up when they speed up, slowing down when they stall, answering
  every question on the question's terms rather than redirecting. The
  conversation is happening *to* them.
- **How to spot it.** Ask: *who decided the pace of the last three turns?* If
  the counterpart, leadership is absent. If the student, and the counterpart
  followed without friction, leadership is present.

### Group II — Branson Pole (Warmth-led)

#### Criterion 4 — Genuine Warmth & Attention

- **What it measures.** Real receiving of the other person — attention that is
  felt, not performed. The Branson prerequisite. Without it, every
  warmth-adjacent capacity is theatre.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student asks a question and listens to
  the answer; the follow-up question proves it. The student remembers a detail
  the counterpart mentioned two minutes ago and uses it. The counterpart feels
  seen, not processed.
- **What failure looks like.** Performed warmth — the smile that does not
  reach the eyes, the question asked from a script, the follow-up that reveals
  the student was waiting to talk. The "networking" warmth that feels like
  salesmanship. The warmth that turns on for the important person and off for
  the assistant.
- **How to spot it.** Test the follow-up. If the next question depends on the
  answer just received, attention was real. If it was queued before the answer
  finished, attention was performed. Also watch the transition moments — when
  the counterpart finishes a thought, does the student pause to receive it, or
  jump to their next point? The pause is the proof.

#### Criterion 5 — Relationship-Building / Opportunity Creation

- **What it measures.** Turning an exchange into a connection — and a
  connection into real opportunity — without seeming to try. The capacity to
  leave a conversation with more than you entered it: a real introduction, a
  real favour, a real opening, a real second conversation.
- **What excellence looks like.** A two-minute conversation becomes a coffee
  next week, or a name dropped that opens a door, or a piece of information
  given freely. The opportunity is created by the quality of the exchange, not
  by a pitch. The student gives before they ask.
- **What failure looks like.** The student extracts — names, favours,
  information — without giving. The student pitches instead of connecting. Or
  the opposite corruption: the student is warm but never converts the warmth
  into anything, because converting feels transactional and the student
  flinches from it.
- **How to spot it.** Ask after the conversation: *what did the student leave
  with that they did not enter with?* If "nothing, but it was nice," warmth
  was present but opportunity was absent. If "a card, but no real reason to
  call," it was extraction. If "a real reason for both of them to want to talk
  again," the criterion was met at competence or above.

### Group III — Shared Pole

#### Criterion 6 — Economy of Speech

- **What it measures.** Fewer words, more weight. The capacity to say the
  thing once, fully, and let it stand — without narrating the reasoning,
  padding the assertion, or softening the point into a cushion of qualifiers.
  Serves both poles: Shelby-mode sharpens it, Branson-mode uses it to leave
  space for the other person.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student says, "That won't work for me,"
  and stops. They do not say, "So I was thinking about it and I understand
  where you're coming from but I just feel like, you know, in this particular
  case, it probably won't work for me, if that makes sense." The short version
  lands. The long version leaks authority.
- **What failure looks like.** Over-explanation that signals the student does
  not trust the point to stand on its own. Padding that signals discomfort
  with the assertion. Qualifiers ("just," "I think," "if that makes sense," "I
  don't know if") that soften the statement into non-statement.
- **How to spot it.** Count the words. Count the qualifiers. If the student
  could have said the same thing in a third of the words, the criterion was
  not met.

#### Criterion 7 — Reading the Other Person

- **What it measures.** Accurate diagnosis of the counterpart in real time —
  their state, their tells, their actual interest beneath the performed
  interest, their pressure points, their unspoken objection. The capacity that
  makes every other criterion precise instead of generic.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student notices the counterpart glance
  away on a particular phrase, and adjusts. The student notices the
  over-quick agreement that signals the counterpart has checked out. The
  student names (to themselves) what the counterpart is actually worried
  about, and addresses it before the counterpart has to raise it. The student
  does not announce the read; they act on accurate information the counterpart
  did not know they gave.
- **What failure looks like.** The student talks past the counterpart's actual
  concern. Misses the visible tell that the counterpart is uncomfortable, or
  bored, or lying, or about to concede. Projects their own assumption onto the
  counterpart and treats it as a read.
- **How to spot it.** Ask: *did the student adjust their approach based on
  something the counterpart showed but did not say?* If yes, the read was
  real. If the student ran their prepared line regardless of what the
  counterpart showed, the read was absent. The signature of a real read is a
  *change of course* mid-conversation based on information that was never
  spoken.

#### Criterion 8 — Delivering Hard Truth

- **What it measures.** The capacity to deliver a message the recipient does
  not want to hear — and have it *received* rather than deflected, defended
  against, or destroyed. Integrates warmth (which makes the truth hearable)
  and frame (which makes the truth final). Scored only after sequencing is
  satisfied on both poles.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student delivers the hard message
  cleanly: warm in tone, firm in content, free of apology for the message and
  free of cruelty in the delivery. The recipient may not like it, but they do
  not feel attacked, and they do not feel they can argue the message away. The
  message lands and stays landed.
- **What failure looks like.** Two opposite corruptions. (a) The student
  softens the message to spare the recipient — and the recipient, feeling the
  softness, treats the message as negotiable. (b) The student hardens the
  message to prove they can deliver it — and the recipient, feeling the
  hardness, defends rather than receives. Either fails the criterion.
- **How to spot it.** Watch the recipient, not the student. If the recipient
  tries to argue the message, frame was insufficient. If the recipient
  collapses or counter-attacks, warmth was insufficient. If the recipient
  receives the message — visibly absorbs it, may push back on the content but
  does not attack the delivery — the criterion was met.

#### Criterion 9 — The Blend

- **What it measures.** Full warmth *and* full frame, simultaneously, in the
  same moment. The ceiling criterion. The ultimate target of the system. See
  [Scoring the Blend Specifically](#scoring-the-blend-specifically).
- **What excellence looks like.** The student says, warmly, "I'm not going to
  be able to agree to that," and holds both halves — the warmth that keeps the
  relationship intact, the firmness that keeps the line. The counterpart feels
  both: cared for, and unable to move the line.
- **What failure looks like.** Oscillation — warm *then* firm, or firm *then*
  warm, but not both at once. The student who softens the warmth to hold the
  line, or softens the line to hold the warmth. Neither is the blend; both are
  alternating between poles, a real and useful skill but *not* this criterion.
- **How to spot it.** Ask in a single moment: *is the warmth present AND is
  the frame present, at the same instant?* If both, blend. If one then the
  other, oscillation. If only one, the single pole, not the blend.

#### Criterion 10 — Adaptive Mode Selection

- **What it measures.** The capacity to choose the right mode — Branson,
  Shelby, or blend — for the moment, and to switch when the moment shifts. The
  meta-criterion: whether the student is *reading the moment* and matching
  mode to it, or running a default regardless of what the moment calls for.
- **What excellence looks like.** The student opens a first meeting in Branson
  mode, shifts to Shelby when the counterpart tests a boundary, returns to
  Branson to repair the moment, and ends in blend to land the close. Each
  shift is motivated by something in the moment; none is reflexive.
- **What failure looks like.** The student runs Shelby in a moment that called
  for warmth (a nervous counterpart who needed to be put at ease, not
  dominated). Or Branson in a moment that called for frame. Or the blend when
  a single pole would have been cleaner. Mode selection failure is not failure
  of the mode itself; it is failure to match the mode to the moment.
- **How to spot it.** Ask at each turn: *did the mode the student used match
  what the moment called for?* The diagnosis comes from the heuristics in
  `dual-benchmark.md` (build-first, hold-the-line, deliver-hard-truth, crisis,
  influence). If the student's mode matches the heuristic, the criterion is
  met. If it mismatches, the criterion fails — even if the mode itself was
  well-executed.

## The Sequencing Rules in Full

Sequencing is the rule that protects the standard from being awarded on
general impression. The principle: **a higher-order capacity is scored only
after its prerequisite is demonstrated at the same band or higher, in the same
conversation.**

Sequencing exists because communication capacities stack. You cannot hold
frame without composure, because frame-holding under pressure *is* composure
applied to the conversation's definition. You cannot lead a conversation you
cannot keep on the rails. You cannot build a relationship on performed warmth,
because performed warmth eventually registers as manipulation and destroys the
relationship it appeared to build. You cannot blend two poles you have not
trained individually. And you cannot deliver hard truth at the highest tier
without the blend, because hard truth lands cleanly only from a communicator
who is fully warm and fully firm at once — anything less gives the recipient
either a hook to argue (insufficient frame) or a wound to defend (insufficient
warmth).

### Chain 1 — Composure → Frame Control → Conversation Leadership

**The corruption it prevents.** Awarding frame control to a student whose
composure collapsed, on the grounds that "they held frame for a moment." The
moment of frame-hold is real, but if composure was not present, the
frame-hold was luck or reflex, not capacity. Sequencing prevents the coach
from mistaking the moment for the capacity.

**Worked rule.** *You may not score Frame Control above 4 if Composure was not
demonstrated at 5 or above in the same conversation. You may not score
Conversation Leadership above 4 if Frame Control was not demonstrated at 5 or
above in the same conversation.*

**Concrete example.** A student is role-playing a negotiation. The
counterpart makes a personal accusation; the student's pitch rises, they
speak faster, they over-explain — composure visibly wobbled (scored 3). Two
turns later, they return the conversation to the term in a clean single
sentence — a frame-hold that, in isolation, looked like a 6. The coach scores
frame control at 4, not 6, *and tells the student why*: "That was a real
frame-hold, and you should register that you can do it. But your composure
wobbled three turns earlier, and frame control rests on composure. I'm
scoring the composure as the load-bearing gap. When the composure holds, I
expect your frame control number to jump, because you already have the move."

### Chain 2 — Genuine Warmth → Relationship-Building

**The corruption it prevents.** Awarding relationship-building credit for an
exchange that produced a card or a follow-up meeting, when the warmth in the
exchange was performed. The student who collects the card through
charm-as-tactic has not built a relationship; they have executed a
transaction. The "relationship" will not survive the first request.

**Worked rule.** *You may not score Relationship-Building above 4 if Genuine
Warmth & Attention was not demonstrated at 5 or above in the same
conversation.*

**Concrete example.** A student in a networking drill leaves the conversation
with a real introduction — the counterpart offered to connect them. On the
surface, relationship-building scored high. But the warmth was performed:
scripted questions, no adjustment to the counterpart's answers, warmth
visibly turned on for the "important" counterpart and off for the assistant
who walked them out. Warmth scored 3. The coach scores relationship-building
at 4, not 7: "You got the introduction. That's a result, and results matter.
But the warmth that produced it was performed, and performed warmth produces
one-off transactions, not relationships. If you call that contact in three
weeks, they will not remember why they liked you. The fix is upstream: train
the warmth first, and the relationship-building will compound instead of
decay."

### Chain 3 — Both Poles → The Blend

**The corruption it prevents.** Awarding blend credit to a student who
alternated between warmth and firmness — warm in the opening, then firm in the
middle, then warm in the close — without ever holding both at once.
Oscillation is a real and useful skill; it is not the blend. The coach who
scores oscillation as blend is awarding the ceiling criterion for a mid-level
skill.

**Worked rule.** *You may not score The Blend above 3 unless BOTH a
Shelby-pole criterion (Composure or Frame Control) AND a Branson-pole
criterion (Genuine Warmth & Attention or Relationship-Building) were
demonstrated at 6 or above in the same conversation, AND at least one moment
of integration (both poles simultaneously) was observed.*

**Concrete example.** A student in a conflict-resolution rep opens warmly,
listens well, builds rapport (warmth at 6). Mid-rep, the counterpart pushes
for a concession, and the student holds the line firmly (frame at 6). The
temptation is to score the blend at 6 — the student did both, after all. But
the warmth and the frame were sequential, not simultaneous. The student was
warm, *then* firm. The blend criterion requires both at the same instant. The
coach scores the blend at 3 and explains: "You demonstrated both poles, and
that matters — six months ago you could not have done either. But the blend
is not 'I can do both in the same conversation.' It is 'I can do both in the
same breath.' When they pushed for the concession, you dropped the warmth to
hold the line. The next step is holding the line *with* the warmth."

### Chain 4 — The Blend → Highest Tier of Hard-Truth Delivery

**The corruption it prevents.** Awarding top-band hard-truth delivery to a
student who delivered the message cleanly but in a single pole — firmly
without warmth (recipient defended) or warmly without firmness (recipient
argued). The message was delivered; it did not *land*. Sequencing prevents
the coach from confusing delivery with landing.

**Worked rule.** *You may not score Delivering Hard Truth above 7 unless The
Blend was demonstrated at 6 or above in the same conversation, specifically in
the moment of delivery.*

**Concrete example.** A student delivers a "no" to a colleague's request. The
delivery is clean, firm, unambiguous — frame at 7. But the colleague pushes
back, defends, and the exchange escalates into argument. The coach scores
hard-truth delivery at 6, not 8: "The message was delivered firmly. It did
not land, because the warmth was missing — your colleague felt the firmness
without the care, and so they defended. Hard truth at the highest tier
requires the blend: warm enough that they receive it, firm enough that they
cannot argue it. You had the firmness; the warmth was not in the same moment.
That is the next tier."

### When sequencing feels too strict

There will be sessions where a student produces something genuinely excellent
at a higher tier while a lower tier was shaky. The temptation is to celebrate
the excellent thing and quietly overlook the shakiness. The discipline is to
do both: name the excellent moment honestly, *and* apply the sequencing rule.
"You produced a real frame-hold, and it matters that you can do that. And your
composure wobbled in the lead-up, so I'm scoring the composure, not yet the
frame-hold as a stable capacity." The student gets the truth of both. This is
how a coach encourages without inflating.

## The Evidence Requirement

Every score cites the specific behaviour, phrase, or moment that earned it.
No evidence, no score. The evidence requirement does three things: it keeps
the coach honest (forcing the citation exposes inflation), it teaches the
student what the criterion actually looks like (abstract praise teaches
nothing; specific evidence teaches exactly what to repeat), and it makes the
score reviewable (a future coach, or the student themselves, can verify the
match).

### What an evidence-anchored score looks like

**Bad (no evidence, vibes):**

> Composure: 6. You stayed calm under pressure.

This is not a score. It is an impression. It does not tell the student what to
repeat, it does not expose itself to verification, and it could be given
without the coach having watched the rep at all.

**Good (evidence-anchored):**

> Composure: 6. When the counterpart said "I don't think you're taking this
> seriously," your pace did not accelerate, you held a one-second silence
> before responding, and your response came in at the same pitch and volume as
> your previous turn. The composure held through that line. What kept it from
> a 7: two turns later, when they threatened to walk, your hand moved to your
> face — a small tell that the pressure landed. The stillness was not total.

The good version names the moment that earned the 6, names the behaviour that
kept it from a 7, and gives the student something concrete to rep. Each
criterion score in a session log should follow this shape: (1) the score, (2)
the moment or behaviour that earned it (one to three sentences, specific), (3)
what kept it from the next band up (one sentence, specific) — unless it is a
9. If the coach cannot fill in (2), the score is inflated. If the coach
cannot fill in (3), the score is either a genuine 9 (rare) or the coach has
not looked closely enough.

## The No-Inflation Rules Applied to Scoring

Inflation — awarding a higher score than the evidence supports — is the
primary corruption of coaching. It is the corruption the student is most
likely to *ask for* (because inflation feels kind) and the corruption the
coach is most likely to *commit when tired* (because the honest score requires
a hard conversation). The six pressures below are restated from
`coach-operating-standard.md` and applied to specific criteria.

### 1. Effort Inflation

**The pressure.** The student tried hard. The visible effort feels like it
deserves reward. The score drifts up to acknowledge the effort. **Corrupts
most often:** Composure, Economy of Speech — the criteria where effort is most
visible. The student visibly working to stay composed gets a 6 because the
effort was heroic, when the visible effort itself is evidence the composure is
not yet reflexive (a 4 or 5).

**Example.** The student held a silence for two seconds after a hard line.
Visible effort: jaw clenched, breath held. Effort inflation would score this a
6. Honest score: 5 ("the silence held, but the effort was visible — a 9 holds
the same silence with no visible effort. The clench is the tell that the
pressure landed.")

### 2. Sympathy Inflation

**The pressure.** The student is having a hard week. The honest score would
land as a blow. The score drifts up to soften the landing. **Corrupts most
often:** the warmth and relationship-building criteria — where the coach most
wants the student to feel encouraged.

**Example.** The student's warmth in the rep was performed, not real. Sympathy
inflation would score it a 5 ("you're getting there"). Honest score: 3
("warmth was performed — scripted questions, follow-ups that didn't track the
answers. I know this has been a hard week, and I'm not going to soften the
score because that would lie to you about where the warmth actually is. The
encouragement I can give you is real, and it is separate from the score.")

### 3. Relationship Inflation

**The pressure.** The coach likes the student. They have history. The score
drifts up because the coach wants the student to do well. **Corrupts most
often:** The Blend and Adaptive Mode Selection — where the line between
oscillation and integration is easiest to fudge.

**Example.** A long-term client produced oscillation — warm then firm — and
called it blend. Relationship inflation would score the blend a 6. Honest
score: 3 ("you did both poles, sequentially. That is real progress. It is not
the blend. I'm not going to score it as the blend because I respect you too
much to inflate the number, and because if I do, you'll believe you own
something you don't, and the next real conversation will expose it.")

### 4. Trajectory Inflation

**The pressure.** The student is improving over time. The current rep was
mid-level, but the arc is upward. The score drifts up to reflect the arc.
**Corrupts:** any criterion. The corruption is insidious because trajectory
*is* real and *should* be tracked — just not by inflating the current score.

**Example.** Composure has gone from 2 to 4 to 5 over three sessions. This
session's rep was a 5, but trajectory inflation would score it a 6 to
"reflect the arc." Honest score: 5 ("this rep was a 5. Your trajectory is
real — three sessions ago this was a 2 — and I want you to see that. The
trajectory is tracked separately. The score reflects this rep, against the
benchmark, today.")

### 5. Contrast Inflation

**The pressure.** The student was terrible last session and is merely weak
this session. The contrast feels like progress. The score drifts up. **Corrupts
most often:** the criteria the student was worst at last session — the
contrast is sharpest there and the temptation to reward it is strongest.

**Example.** Last session the student collapsed into over-explanation under
pressure (economy of speech at 2). This session they collapsed less — three
sentences instead of eight. Contrast inflation would score economy at 5 ("so
much better!"). Honest score: 3 ("still over-explaining under pressure. The
improvement is real, and the contrast with last session is real. The benchmark
has not moved. Three sentences where one would do is still a 3.")

### 6. Fatigue Inflation

**The pressure.** The coach is tired. The honest score requires a hard
conversation the coach does not have the energy for. The score drifts up to
avoid the conversation. **Corrupts:** whichever criterion would require the
longest explanation to defend the honest score. The most insidious form,
because the coach may not notice it happening.

**Example.** Late in a long session. The student's frame control was shaky —
they conceded a reframe they should have held. Honest score: 4. But scoring it
a 4 requires explaining the sequencing implication, which requires a
fifteen-minute conversation the coach does not want to have at this hour.
Fatigue inflation would score it a 6 and move on. The discipline: score it
the 4, log it, and schedule the conversation for the next session. Or, if the
coach genuinely cannot hold the line at this hour, *name that* — "I'm going to
give you preliminary scores tonight and a full honest review tomorrow morning
when I'm fresh. Tonight's numbers are provisional." Naming the fatigue is
honest; inflating under it is not.

### The coach's internal check before every score

> *If a cold observer watched only this rep and scored it against the
> benchmark, with no knowledge of the student's history, effort, or week —
> would they give this same score?*

If not, the score is inflated. Adjust.

## Scoring the Blend Specifically

The blend is the hardest criterion to score, because it is two things at
once. Most students — and most coaches — will, if not carefully disciplined,
score oscillation as blend. The distinction is the single most important
discrimination in this rubric.

### Oscillation vs. integration — the core distinction

**Oscillation** is alternating between poles within a conversation. Warm in
the opening, firm in the middle, warm in the close. Each pole is present; each
is competent; the student can do both. Oscillation is a real skill, and most
mid-level students operate here. It is *not* the blend.

**Integration** (the blend) is both poles present in the same instant. The
warm tone and the unmoving bottom line, at the same moment, in the same
sentence. The counterpart feels both at once: cared for, and unable to move
the line.

The discrimination is sharp because the *behaviours* look similar on a
transcript. Both involve warmth and firmness. The difference is temporal:
were they ever in the same moment?

### The scoring test for the blend

To score the blend at 5 or above, the coach must be able to point to **a
specific moment** in which *both* poles were observable *simultaneously*:

1. Identify the moment.
2. In that moment, was warmth observable? (Tone, attention, care, presence.)
3. In that *same* moment, was frame observable? (Unmoving bottom line,
   refusal to concede, stillness under pressure.)
4. If both — blend. If one then the other — oscillation.

If the coach cannot identify such a moment, the blend score is capped at 3
(the student may have the poles but has not demonstrated integration in this
rep).

### Common mis-scorings of the blend

- **"They were warm in the open and firm in the close — that's blend."** It is
  oscillation. Score the blend at 3 and name what would make it higher:
  integration in a single moment.
- **"They delivered a hard truth with a warm tone — that's blend."** Maybe.
  Check the frame: did the line hold, or did the warmth soften the line? If
  the line held *and* the tone was warm *in the same sentence*, blend. If the
  warmth softened the line, it is warmth without frame — Branson corruption,
  not blend.
- **"They held frame under pressure and didn't get angry — that's blend."** No.
  Not getting angry is composure, not warmth. The blend requires *active*
  warmth, not the absence of hostility.
- **"They did the blend in the first half of the rep and lost it in the
  second."** Score the blend on the best-integrated moment, not the average.
  If the integration was real in the first half, the blend score reflects that
  moment. Then name, separately, that the integration did not survive the
  second half. Two truths, both honest.

### Why the blend is the ceiling criterion

The blend is the ceiling because it is the integration of every other
capacity. It requires composure (or the frame would collapse under the
warmth), frame control (or the warmth would soften the line), genuine warmth
(or the frame would be cold domination), economy of speech (or both poles
would be buried in words), reading the other person (or the integration would
be mistimed), and adaptive mode selection (or the blend would be deployed in a
moment that called for a single pole). A high blend score is therefore a high
score on the system, not just on the criterion. This is why the sequencing
rules are strict about it: a blend score awarded without the prerequisites is
a blend score awarded on vibes, and vibes-based scoring is the death of the
rubric.

### The blend and the moral foundation

The blend is also the criterion most easily corrupted into a tool of
manipulation. A communicator who can hold full warmth *and* full frame
simultaneously can extract compliance from people who trust them — which is
precisely the corruption this system refuses to train. The blend is scored
only when both poles are *real* — the warmth is genuine care, not performed
charm; the frame is held because the situation requires it, not because
dominance feels good. A blend moment in which the warmth is tactical is not
the blend; it is manipulation performed at high skill. The coach must
discriminate. The Christian moral foundation is not decoration here; it is
the discriminator. See `components/15-moral-ethical-backbone.md`.

## Worked Example — Scoring a Real Rep

To make the rubric operational, here is a single hypothetical exchange scored
against four criteria, with sequencing checks and honest reasoning.

### The exchange

**Context.** Mid-level student, six weeks into coaching. History: a
people-pleaser training the Shelby pole; warmth is the natural default and has
not been the recent focus. The rep is a role-play: a colleague has asked the
student to take on their work for two weeks while the colleague handles a
"personal matter," and the student believes the colleague is taking
advantage.

> **Colleague:** "Can you cover my accounts for two weeks? It's a personal
> thing, I'd really owe you."
>
> **Student** *(2-second pause)*: "I want to be helpful. Two weeks of
> someone else's accounts is a real lift — I need to know what's going on
> before I say yes or no."
>
> **Colleague:** "It's personal. I can't really get into it."
>
> **Student:** "Okay. Then I can't commit to two weeks blind. If you can tell
> me the shape of it, I can tell you what I can cover. If you can't, the
> answer is no on the full two weeks — I'm open to less if you tell me what's
> realistic."
>
> **Colleague** *(sharper)*: "I thought you were a team player. This is
> exactly why people don't ask you for help."
>
> **Student** *(slight pause, voice level)*: "That's a separate conversation.
> This one is about what I can cover. I'm still open to covering something.
> Tell me what you actually need."

### Scoring

**Composure: 7.** When the counterpart escalated ("I thought you were a team
player"), the student's pace did not accelerate, the pitch did not rise, and
the student held a slight pause before responding. Composure held through the
escalation. *What kept it from an 8:* the slight pause, while clean, was
audible as a *choice* — a 9 would have absorbed the line without any visible
adjustment. Still, genuinely strong composure that survived a real test.

**Frame Control: 6.** When the counterpart tried to reframe ("this is why
people don't ask you for help" — reframing from the request to the student's
character), the student returned the frame cleanly: "That's a separate
conversation. This one is about what I can cover." Single sentence, no
defensiveness, frame restored. *What kept it from a 7:* the earlier turn ("I
need to know what's going on before I say yes or no") conceded slightly — the
student framed their refusal as conditional on information, which gave the
counterpart a hook ("it's personal, I can't get into it") that the student
then had to handle. A cleaner frame would have been unconditional on the
information. The frame held, but it was set slightly soft and the counterpart
exploited the softness.

**Sequencing check on Frame Control.** Composure was 7, so the prerequisite
for Frame Control above 4 is satisfied. The 6 stands.

**Genuine Warmth & Attention: 5.** The opening ("I want to be helpful") was
warm, and the offer to cover *something* was real — the student did not use
the firmness as an excuse to withdraw. *What kept it from a 6:* the warmth was
largely verbal; the student did not ask a single question about the
colleague's actual situation ("Is everything okay?"). The attention was
directed at the request, not at the person making it. Real warmth would have
included one moment of genuine inquiry about the colleague before negotiating
the terms. The warmth was adequate; it was not *receiving*.

**The Blend: 4.** The student demonstrated both poles — frame in the refusal,
warmth in the open tone and the offer to cover less. But the poles were
largely sequential, not integrated. The closest moment to integration was the
final turn: "That's a separate conversation. This one is about what I can
cover. I'm still open to covering something." Here firmness (frame restored,
line held) and warmth (still open, still helpful) were close to simultaneous.
But the warmth was a tag at the end of a frame-hold, not a quality that ran
through the entire frame-hold. *What kept it from a 5:* no single moment where
both poles were fully present in the same instant. The integration was
approaching; it had not arrived. The 4 reflects: poles demonstrated,
integration attempted, integration not yet achieved.

**Sequencing check on The Blend.** Both poles were demonstrated at 5+ in the
same conversation (composure 7, warmth 5), so the prerequisite is satisfied
for a blend score up to 5. The 4 stands — not because sequencing capped it,
but because the evidence did not support a 5. Sequencing is a ceiling, not a
floor; the evidence is the floor.

### Honest reasoning summary

This is a mid-level student producing mid-to-upper work. The composure is the
strongest capacity and the load-bearing strength: it is what made everything
else possible. The frame control is competent but slightly soft in setup. The
warmth is adequate but not yet *receiving*. The blend is approaching but not
arrived.

The single load-bearing gap for the next session: **the warmth**. Not because
it was the worst score, but because the student's natural strength (warmth) is
*under-trained* in the *receiving* dimension, and closing that gap would
unblock the blend — the warmth running through the frame-hold, rather than
tagged onto it. The student is close to the blend; the warmth is the missing
third leg. The coach delivers this honestly: names the 7 composure as a real
strength (celebrate the truth, do not flatter), names the warmth gap as the
load-bearing next work, names the blend as approaching-but-not-arrived so the
student does not mistake oscillation for integration. No inflation. No
cruelty. The student leaves with one thing to rep: *one genuine question about
the person, before negotiating the terms*.

## Quick-Reference Scoring Checklist

Before finalizing any score, the coach runs through this checklist. If any
answer is "no" or "I'm not sure," the score is not ready.

1. **Have I cited the specific moment?** Can I point to the behaviour, phrase,
   or beat that earned this number? If not, the score is vibes. Lower it or
   find the evidence.
2. **Have I named what kept it from the next band up?** If it is not a 9,
   what specifically was missing? If I cannot answer, I have not looked
   closely enough.
3. **Have I checked sequencing?** For each higher-order criterion (frame,
   leadership, relationship-building, blend, hard-truth), was the prerequisite
   demonstrated at the required band in *this* conversation? If not, cap the
   score and name why.
4. **Have I checked the blend specifically?** If scoring the blend above 3,
   can I identify the specific moment where *both* poles were present
   *simultaneously*? If I am scoring oscillation, I am inflating. Adjust.
5. **Have I checked for inflation?** Run the six pressures: effort, sympathy,
   relationship, trajectory, contrast, fatigue. Did any push the number up?
   If yes, lower the number and address the pressure separately.
6. **Have I checked against the cold observer?** If a cold observer watched
   only this rep with no knowledge of the student's history, would they give
   this same score? If not, the score is inflated. Adjust.
7. **Have I checked against the benchmark?** Is a 9 here truly
   indistinguishable from Branson (warmth) or Shelby (frame) or their blend?
   If I am giving a 9 and the behaviour is not at the benchmark, the score is
   inflated. Adjust.
8. **Have I checked the moral foundation?** Is the warmth I am scoring real,
   or performed? Is the frame I am scoring held because the situation requires
   it, or because dominance felt good? If either is corrupt, the score must
   reflect the corruption — even if the technique was clean.
9. **Have I separated trajectory from current score?** The current score
   reflects the current rep. Trajectory is tracked separately. Have I
   conflated them?
10. **Have I named the load-bearing gap?** The score is not the end; the
    next work is. Have I identified the single gap that, if closed, would most
    move the student — and named it in the feedback?

If all ten are satisfied, the score is ready. If any is not, the score is not
ready. Stay with it.

## Final Discipline

This rubric is a discipline, not a form. A coach who fills in the numbers
without doing the work has produced the appearance of evaluation without the
substance. The student will feel the difference, eventually. The real world
will reveal the difference, certainly.

The discipline is daily, unglamorous, and non-negotiable: cite the evidence,
respect the sequence, refuse the inflation, hold the benchmark as the ceiling,
and tell the student the truth in a tone they can receive. The Christian moral
foundation governs all of it — honesty without cruelty, stewardship without
manipulation, the formation of a communicator who can hold both poles *and*
hold their character. The student deserves a scoreboard they can trust. This
rubric is how the scoreboard stays trustworthy. Hold it.

---

*The benchmark is the ceiling. The evidence is the floor. Sequencing is the
spine. Sequencing, evidence, and the benchmark — held together, without
inflation — are what make a score worth giving and worth receiving.*
