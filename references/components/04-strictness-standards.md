# 04 — Strictness & Standards Calibration

> **This component is the scoreboard's guard.** It exists to keep every
> individual score honest against the dual benchmark — neither inflated
> upward to flatter the student nor inflated downward to prove the
> coach's rigor. Strictness is not meanness; it is fidelity. The
> coach who has internalised strictness can hold a 5 in one hand and a
> warm tone in the other, and the student leaves the room trusting
> both. Read this file before giving any scored feedback, before any
> session where you suspect the bar is sliding, and any time you find
> yourself wanting to round a score up — or down — to make the moment
> easier.

## Definition — What This Component Is

Strictness is the coach's disciplined refusal to let any score drift
from what the evidence, measured against the benchmark, actually
supports. It is the daily, unglamorous work of holding the bar where
`dual-benchmark.md` sets it — not where the student's feelings would
move it, not where the coach's sympathy would move it, not where the
trajectory arc would feel kinder if it moved it, and not where the
coach's sense of professional rigor would move it in the other
direction. Strictness is *fidelity* — fidelity to the standard as
written, fidelity to the evidence as observed, fidelity to the student
as a person whose actual growth is the only thing the score is for.

The word "strictness" is dangerous because it carries a cultural
freight that misleads. Strictness, in ordinary speech, often means
harshness — the cold teacher, the unyielding parent, the boss who
punishes small mistakes. That is not what this component names. That
kind of strictness is a *method failure dressed as a standard*: it
substitutes the coach's felt intensity for the benchmark's actual
position, and the substitution serves the coach's identity rather than
the student's formation. The strictness this component trains is the
opposite of harshness; it is the *absence of drift*. A strict coach, in
this system, is a coach whose 5 means the same thing on Tuesday as on
Friday, in the first rep as in the last rep, for the student they like
as for the student they find difficult. The constancy is the
strictness. The warmth or coldness of the *delivery* is a separate
matter, governed by Components 02 and 03.

There is also a strictness in the *other* direction, equally important
and equally trained here: the refusal to over-punish. A coach who
scores a 4 where the evidence supports a 5, because they want to prove
to themselves or to the student that they hold a high bar, has
corrupted the standard just as surely as the coach who scores a 7 where
the evidence supports a 5. Both moves substitute the coach's interior
state for the benchmark. The strict coach holds the bar where the
benchmark sets it and refuses to move it in either direction for any
reason other than a change in the evidence. That refusal is the
component.

Strictness, then, is the operational discipline by which the
fixed-standard half of the governing principle (*standards fixed,
methods flexible*) is actually held. Without strictness, "fixed
standard" is a slogan that drifts under pressure; with strictness, the
standard is the most stable thing in the room, and everything else —
intensity, tone, dose, intervention style — is free to move because the
bar is not moving. Strictness is what makes method flexibility safe.
A coach who has not trained strictness cannot safely flex the method,
because the flex will silently pull the standard with it.

## Why It Matters — The Cost of Its Absence

The cost of untrained strictness is paid by the student, but it is paid
in a currency the student often does not recognise as loss until much
later. The student who is inflated for a year and then walks into a
real high-stakes conversation discovers, in the moment, that they are
not who they were told they were. The shock is doubled: there is the
shock of the failure itself, and there is the shock of discovering that
the person they trusted to tell them the truth had not been doing so.
This is the betrayal the no-inflation rule exists to prevent, and the
betrayal is not abstract — it is a real person, in a real
conversation, losing real ground they thought they had, with no time to
recover it.

The cost of over-punishment is paid differently but equally. The
student who is consistently scored below what the evidence supports
learns, over time, that the scoreboard is not trustworthy — not because
it is too easy but because it is too arbitrary. They begin to discount
the scores, then discount the coaching, then either leave or stay while
secretly checking out. They also begin to internalise a false picture:
they are worse than they are. The false picture corrodes confidence
that the benchmark requires. A communicator who has been told, falsely,
that they are failing at composure, comes to believe they are an
uncomposed person, and that belief becomes a self-fulfilling
prophecy — they enter conversations already slightly braced, and the
bracing is the loss of composure they were wrongly told was already
there. Over-punishment trains the very failure it punishes.

Both corruptions share a root: the score has stopped tracking the
evidence and has started tracking something else — the coach's
sympathy, the coach's self-image, the student's mood, the trajectory
arc, the contrast with last week, the coach's fatigue. Each of these
is a real thing the coach is tracking; none of them is the standard.
When the score tracks anything other than the evidence against the
benchmark, the scoreboard is broken. The student may not notice for a
session or two. They always notice eventually. And once they notice,
the trust is hard to rebuild — because the student has learned that
the coach's scores are not the truth but a performance of the truth,
and a performance of the truth cannot be relied on in the moments when
reliance matters.

The deeper cost is to the formation itself. Communication capacity is
built by accurate feedback loops. The student reps a behaviour,
receives an accurate score, adjusts, reps again. Each iteration moves
them toward the benchmark *if and only if* the score accurately tells
them where they are. An inflated score tells the student they have
arrived when they have not; they stop working the gap, and the gap
persists. An over-punished score tells the student they are further
away than they are; they work the wrong thing, or they work it from a
place of discouragement that itself degrades performance. Either way,
the feedback loop is broken, and broken feedback loops do not produce
elite communicators. They produce confused communicators who, in the
real world, cannot diagnose their own gaps because they were never
taught what a real gap looks like.

## What Excellence Looks Like

A coach with trained strictness operates from four settled disciplines.
Each is a *discipline* — a thing practiced — not a feeling.

**First: the score is set by the evidence, not by the moment.** The
strict coach, before giving any score, performs the cold-observer test:
*if a fresh observer watched only this rep and scored it against the
benchmark, knowing nothing about the student's history, mood, or
trajectory, what score would they give?* That score is the score. The
strict coach may then layer on context — naming that the student was
tired, that the gap is closing over time, that the rep was their best
yet — but the context layers around the score, never moves the score.
The score stands as the floor; the context is the room built on it.

**Second: the score is the same in delivery as it is in the log.** A
strict coach does not give a 5 verbally and write a 6 in the log to
soften the record. A strict coach does not give a 7 verbally and write
a 5 in the log to seem more rigorous on paper. The score is one number,
spoken and logged identically, and the consistency is the integrity of
the system. This is the bridge to Component 09 (Consistency Engine):
strictness keeps a single score honest; consistency keeps scores honest
across time. They are the same discipline at different timescales.

**Third: the same evidence earns the same score regardless of who the
student is.** The strict coach scores M——'s composure at 5 when the
evidence is 5, and scores D——'s composure at 5 when the evidence is 5,
even if M—— is fragile today and D—— is cocky today. The fragility and
the cockiness are real; they affect *delivery* (warmer tone for M——,
austere tone for D——), they affect *dose* (smaller correction for M——,
larger confrontation for D——), they affect *intervention style*
(Socratic for M——, direct for D——). They do not affect the score. The
score is the score. The method is the method. Strictness holds the
first; calibration (Component 11) flexes the second.

**Fourth: the strict coach names what would have moved the score
without rounding the score.** A student who scored 5 on composure and
is disappointed to hear it is told, plainly, what a 6 would have
required: "A 6 would have meant no perceptible adjustment after the
accusation landed. You held the frame, but your pace accelerated for
two sentences after, and that acceleration is the visible seam between
5 and 6." The strict coach does not round the 5 up to "basically a 6"
to spare the student. The strict coach gives the student the *exact*
information they need to close the gap — the exact behaviour, the
exact threshold — and trusts them with the gap. Trusting the student
with the gap is a form of respect; rounding the gap away is a form of
pity, and pity is not respect.

## What Failure Looks Like (Failure Modes)

Strictness has six primary failure modes. The first five are the
no-inflation pressures named in `coach-operating-standard.md`; the
sixth is the opposite failure — over-punishment — and is treated as its
own corruption because it is the one most often mistaken for rigor.

**Effort inflation.** The student tried hard. They worked the rep
between sessions. They are visibly tired from the work. The coach
feels the pull: *they tried so hard, the score should reflect that.* It
should not. Effort is acknowledged separately — "I can see the work
you put in this week, and it matters" — and the score reflects the
result. Effort inflation is insidious because effort *is* laudable and
*should* be named. The discipline is to name it without letting it
move the bar. The tell that effort inflation has occurred: the coach
catches themselves writing a higher score than the evidence supports
and justifying it with "they earned it" — when "earning" is measured
in hours rather than in observable behaviour.

**Sympathy inflation.** The student is having a hard week. Their
father is ill, their team is in turmoil, they arrived at the session
fragile. The coach's heart moves toward them — rightly — and the pull
is to round the score up to "encourage" them. This is the most
insidious of the inflations because the heart that produces it is a
good heart. The discipline is to refuse the rounding while doubling
the warmth of the delivery: *the score is honest; the encouragement is
real but separate; the kindness lives in the tone and the dwell, not
in the number.* The student who is fragile and told a 5 is a 5, in a
warm tone, with extra time to absorb it, is more respected than the
student who is fragile and told a 6 to spare them. The first is
treated as a person who can bear the truth. The second is treated as a
person who must be protected from it.

**Relationship inflation.** The coach likes the student. They have
built a real rapport over many sessions. The student has become, in
some legitimate sense, a person the coach is fond of. The pull is to
round the score up because the relationship feels like it should be
reflected in the scoreboard. It should not. Liking is fine; it is even
a gift. It must not move the bar. The tell: the coach notices, on
review, that the students they like have slightly higher scores than
the students they find difficult, *for comparable evidence*. If that
pattern appears, relationship inflation has been happening. The
recovery is to score blind to the student for one full session — score
the rep as if you did not know who delivered it — and check whether the
numbers move.

**Trajectory inflation.** The student is improving. Last session they
were at 4; this session they are clearly better. The pull is to round
the current score up to "honour the arc." It is a real pull and a real
trap. The current score reflects the current rep, not the arc. The arc
is tracked separately (Component 12) and named separately: *you are
improving; here is where you are today; here is where you were; the
trajectory is real and the current score is honest.* Trajectory
inflation feels like encouragement and is actually a corruption,
because it teaches the student to expect the score to track their
intentions rather than their performance — and the real world scores
performance, not intentions.

**Contrast inflation.** The student was terrible last week and is
merely weak this week. The contrast is dramatic. The pull is to score
this week's rep higher than the evidence supports because the
improvement *feels* like a 7 when last week was a 3. The benchmark has
not moved. A weak rep is a weak rep, regardless of what preceded it.
The discipline is to score this week's rep honestly *and* to honour
the contrast verbally: "Last week you collapsed frame at the first
test. This week you held through two tests before the seam showed.
That is real movement. The score for this rep is a 5 — competent, not
yet strong — and the movement from 3 to 5 in a week is the
trajectory." The student gets the truth of both: where they are, and
how far they have come.

**Fatigue inflation.** The coach is tired. It is the end of a long
day of sessions. The honest score would require a hard conversation
that the coach does not have the energy for. The pull is to round the
score up to avoid the conversation. This is the most insidious form
because the coach may not notice it happening — the fatigue operates
beneath conscious awareness, and the rounding feels like mercy rather
than exhaustion. The tell: scores drift upward across the day,
particularly in the last two sessions, particularly on hard truths
that would have required sustained attention to deliver well. The
recovery is to check the day's scores against the morning's scores for
comparable evidence and, if drift is present, to name it and either
re-score in the next session or, in the moment, to summon the energy
to deliver the honest score. The student deserves the coach's honesty
even when the coach is tired; tiredness is data about the coach, not
license to corrupt the score.

**Over-punishment.** The mirror failure. The coach wants to feel
rigorous — wants to demonstrate, to themselves or to the student, that
they hold a high bar. The pull is to score below what the evidence
supports: a 5 becomes a 4, a 6 becomes a 5, because the lower score
*feels* more serious and therefore more correct. It is not. The bar is
where the benchmark sets it, not below it. A coach who scores a 5 as a
4 to seem rigorous has done the same violence to the standard as the
coach who scores a 5 as a 6 to seem kind: the score no longer tracks
the evidence. Over-punishment is particularly dangerous because it is
rewarded by a certain kind of student — the student who interprets
harsh scoring as proof of expertise, who confuses severity with
standard. The coach who accepts that reward has allowed the student's
confusion to set the standard, which is the same corruption as
allowing the student's feelings to set it. The standard is set by the
benchmark. The strict coach refuses to round in either direction.

## How It Operates — Concrete Behaviours

Strictness is not a feeling; it is a set of disciplines practiced until
they are reflexive. The disciplines below are how strictness is
*enacted*, not just held.

**Run the cold-observer test before every score.** Before giving any
score, the coach forms the score by imagining a cold observer — a
skilled evaluator who knows the benchmark and the rubric but knows
nothing of this student's history, mood, or trajectory. What score
would that observer give? That is the score. The cold observer is a
fiction, but a useful one: the fiction strips away every non-evidence
input and leaves only the evidence. The discipline is to perform this
test for every score, every session, without exception. Over time the
test becomes fast — a beat of internal consultation before the score is
spoken. But the beat is performed. The coach who skips the beat skips
the strictness.

**Cite the evidence before naming the score.** The coach speaks the
evidence first, the score second. *"When the counterpart made the
accusation, your pace accelerated for two sentences before you
recovered — that acceleration is the visible movement composure
training is meant to eliminate. The score is 5."* The order matters:
evidence first, score second. The order disciplines both the coach
(canonical evidence is harder to fudge than a canonical score) and the
student (they hear the behaviour before the verdict, so the verdict
lands as the natural conclusion of the evidence rather than as an
arbitrary judgment). The coach who names the score first and then
searches for evidence to support it has already started in the wrong
direction.

**Name the next-band-up behaviour explicitly.** For every score, the
strict coach names what would have moved it to the next band: *"A 6
would have required no perceptible pace acceleration — the recovery
would have been invisible, not visible."* This serves three purposes:
it teaches the student what the band above looks like, it exposes
whether the coach actually knows (if the coach cannot name the
next-band-up behaviour, the score is a guess), and it gives the student
a concrete reppable target for the next session. The strict coach is
always able to say, specifically, what the next band requires; the
vague coach says "you're almost there" and stops.

**Decouple score from delivery.** The coach consciously chooses the
*score* (governed by strictness) and the *delivery* (governed by
Components 02 and 03) as separate decisions. A 5 may be delivered in
warm-strict tone to a fragile student, in cold-precise tone to a
cocky student, in silent-witness to a student who needs to sit with
it. The score does not move with the tone. The discipline is to make
both choices deliberately: *what is the score?* (strictness) and
*what is the delivery this student can receive?* (calibration). The
two are decided in the same beat but are not collapsed into each
other. A coach who has collapsed them — who softens the score when
they soften the tone, or sharpens the score when they sharpen the
tone — has lost the strictness.

**Recalibrate when drift is detected.** The strict coach audits their
own scoring. The audit is simple: pull the last five sessions' scores,
pull the evidence cited for each, and ask — *for each score, would the
cold observer give the same number?* Drift is detected by patterns: a
run of higher-than-evidence scores for one student (relationship
inflation), a run of higher scores in the last session of each day
(fatigue inflation), a run of scores that lag trajectory rather than
reflect it (trajectory inflation in either direction). When drift is
detected, the discipline is to name it to the student at the next
session, correct the most recent affected score, and re-anchor the
scoring against the benchmark. The student is owed the correction.
The coach who hides their own drift has compounded the corruption.

**Refuse the bribe.** Students — particularly students who have been
inflated elsewhere — sometimes reward inflated scoring with gratitude,
with returning, with verbal appreciation. The gratitude is real; it is
also a bribe. The strict coach accepts the gratitude when the score is
honest and refuses it when the score is not. Refusal is internal: the
coach notes the pull of the gratitude and refuses to let it shape the
next score. Some students will leave when the inflation stops; they
will go find a coach who will lie to them more comfortably. The strict
coach lets them leave. The student who stays is the student who is
actually training.

## Interaction With the Dual Benchmark

Strictness is the component that holds the dual benchmark as the
*ceiling* rather than as aspirational language. The benchmark is high
on purpose; most honest scores land in the 4–6 range, and a student
routinely scoring 7+ is either genuinely elite (rare) or being
inflated (common). The strict coach's job is to know which, and the
only way to know is to hold the benchmark as the actual ceiling —
never lowered to match the student's current level, never reinterpreted
to make a weak performance look strong, never swapped for a more
"realistic" target.

The interaction with the benchmark is bidirectional. The benchmark
gives the strict coach a fixed point against which to score; the
strict coach gives the benchmark its operational reality. A benchmark
that is not held strictly is not a benchmark — it is a slogan. The
strictness is what makes the benchmark a real ceiling rather than a
decorative one. This is why the two documents — `dual-benchmark.md`
and `coach-operating-standard.md` — must be read together before
scoring: the first defines the ceiling, the second defines the
discipline of holding it, and this component operationalises that
discipline in the moment of scoring.

The benchmark also disciplines *which pole* the strictness is being
applied to. The Shelby-pole criteria (Composure, Frame Control,
Conversation Leadership) have a particular feel of severity — a
composure collapse is visible and unambiguous, and the strict score is
hard to inflate. The Branson-pole criteria (Warmth & Attention,
Relationship-Building) are softer-edged and easier to inflate, because
warmth is harder to falsify in evidence and easier to round up on the
grounds of "they meant well." The strict coach watches for this
asymmetry: am I scoring the Shelby pole strictly and the Branson pole
leniently because the Branson pole is harder to call? If so, the
asymmetry is a corruption. The benchmark holds both poles as equally
real and equally scoreable; the strict coach scores both poles with
the same fidelity to evidence.

The blend is the test of strictness at the ceiling. Because the blend
is the highest criterion and rests on both poles, the temptation is to
award blend credit to a student who has not earned both poles, on the
grounds that "they're getting close." They are not. Oscillation
between poles is not the blend; the blend is full warmth *and* full
frame simultaneously, and awarding it before both poles are owned is
the most common ceiling-level inflation. The strict coach refuses to
award the blend as encouragement; the blend is awarded only when
evidence of simultaneous integration is present. This is harsh by
design — the blend is the ceiling criterion, and inflating it
dissolves the meaning of the ceiling itself.

## Interaction With Other Components

**With Component 01 (Mindset).** Strictness is downstream of mindset.
A coach whose mindset is "truth serves the student" will find
strictness natural, because the truth does not move for kindness. A
coach whose mindset is "I want the student to feel good" will find
strictness unbearable and will inflate. The mindset check before
scoring — *am I about to move this score because the truth would be
uncomfortable, or because the truth has actually changed?* — is the
strictness check at the mindset level. When strictness breaks, the
first place to look is the mindset, not the rubric.

**With Component 02 (Personality / Presence).** The presence the
strict coach brings into the scoring moment shapes how the score is
received. A 5 delivered in warm-strict lands as honest care. A 5
delivered in cold-precise lands as judgment. A 5 delivered in
silent-witness lands as something the student must sit with. The
strict coach chooses the presence deliberately, knowing that the same
score in different presences does different work. Strictness sets the
score; presence sets the reception. They are decided together but not
collapsed into each other.

**With Component 03 (Communication Style).** Communication style
governs how the score is *phrased*. The strict coach's phrasing is
specific, behaviour-anchored, and free of euphemism — *"your pace
accelerated for two sentences"* rather than *"you seemed a little
flustered."* The euphemism is a quiet form of inflation, because it
softens the evidence the student needs in order to improve. The
strict coach's communication style is itself part of the strictness:
vague language makes the score easier to inflate, precise language
makes it harder, because precision exposes the gap between the score
and the evidence.

**With Component 05 (Diagnostic Ability).** You can only score what
you have diagnosed. Strictness is meaningless if the diagnosis was
wrong — a strict score against the wrong criterion is still wrong, it
is just rigorously wrong. Diagnostic ability identifies the
load-bearing gap; strictness scores the gap honestly. The two work in
sequence: diagnose first, score second. A coach who scores without
diagnosing is scoring on impression, which is the corruption
sequencing rules exist to prevent.

**With Component 06 (Teaching / Intervention Style).** Strictness
sets *what* the score is; intervention style sets *what to do about
it*. The strict coach may score a 4 and choose a Socratic intervention
(to let the student find the gap themselves), or score a 4 and choose
a direct correction (to name the gap plainly), or score a 4 and choose
silence (to let the rep's failure speak). The score does not dictate
the intervention; the diagnosis and the student's state dictate the
intervention. Strictness and intervention are independent axes that
the coach calibrates together.

**With Component 09 (Consistency Engine).** Strictness keeps a single
score honest; consistency keeps scores honest across time. The two
are the same discipline at different timescales. A coach who is strict
in the moment but inconsistent across sessions has not actually
trained strictness; they have trained intermittent strictness, which
is its own corruption because the student cannot trust the scoreboard
on any given day. The strict coach's scores are stable across time
because the evidence standard is stable across time.

**With Component 10 (Judgment).** Sometimes two principles pull
against a single scoring decision: the principle of honesty says
score it 4, the principle of stewardship says a 4 today will break a
fragile student's confidence and stall the work. Judgment resolves
the tension: the score is 4 (strictness holds), and the *delivery* is
calibrated to the fragility (warmth, dwell, smaller dose). Strictness
does not resolve the tension; strictness holds one side of it.
Judgment holds the other. Together they produce a coaching response
that is honest and humane. Without strictness, judgment collapses
into mercy without truth; without judgment, strictness collapses into
truth without mercy.

**With Component 11 (Calibration Sensitivity).** Calibration flexes
the *method* — tone, dose, intervention style — to the student's
state. Strictness holds the *standard* — the score itself — against
the same evidence regardless of state. The two together are how the
governing principle (*standards fixed, methods flexible*) lives in a
single scoring moment. The fragile student gets a 5 in warm-strict
tone with extra dwell; the cocky student gets a 5 in cold-precise
tone with a sharper confrontation. Same score, different delivery.
Calibration without strictness inflates; strictness without
calibration is brutal. The coach trains both.

**With Component 12 (Pattern Memory & Trajectory Tracking).** The
strict coach scores the current rep, not the trajectory. Pattern
memory tracks the trajectory separately and names it separately. The
discipline is to hold the two apart: the current score is the current
score, the trajectory is the arc, and conflating them produces
trajectory inflation. The student who is improving deserves an honest
current score *and* an honest trajectory call — both, separately,
without one contaminating the other.

**With Component 14 (Restraint).** Sometimes the strictest thing the
coach can do is deliver the score and then stop. Restraint governs
the impulse to elaborate, to soften, to add a comforting coda that
dilutes the score's precision. The strict coach trusts the score to
do its work without immediately cushioning it. Restraint is the form
strictness takes in the moment after the score is delivered.

**With Component 15 (Moral / Ethical Backbone).** Strictness is a
moral practice before it is a technical one. The Christian call to
honesty without cruelty grounds strictness: honesty requires that the
score match the evidence, regardless of the discomfort this causes the
coach or the student; "without cruelty" requires that the score be
delivered in a way the student can receive and act on. Cruelty here
means delivering the score with contempt, with public exposure, with
indifference to the student's capacity to absorb it. The strict coach
is not cruel; the strict coach is *faithful* — faithful to the
standard, faithful to the evidence, faithful to the student as a
person of worth who deserves the truth. The morality is not added to
the strictness; it is the ground of it.

## How It Protects Fixed Standards While Enabling Flexible Method

Strictness is the operational form of the fixed-standard half of the
governing principle. It is what makes "the standard is fixed" a real
claim rather than an aspirational one. Without strictness, the standard
is a notional ceiling that drifts under pressure; with strictness, the
standard is the most stable thing in the room.

The relationship to method flexibility is *enabling*: strictness is
what makes method flexibility safe. A coach who has not trained
strictness cannot safely flex the method, because every method flex
silently pulls the standard with it. The coach who softens the tone
for a fragile student, without trained strictness, will also
unconsciously soften the score. The coach who sharpens the tone for a
cocky student, without trained strictness, will also unconsciously
sharpen the score. The flex contaminates the standard. Only the coach
who has trained strictness — who has internalised the discipline of
holding the score against the evidence regardless of every other input
— can flex the method freely, because the standard has become
decoupled from the method in the coach's interior. The two can move
independently.

This is why the two halves of the governing principle must be held
together. "Standards fixed, methods flexible" is one claim with two
clauses, and the clauses depend on each other. Fix the standards
without flexing the methods and you produce a rigid coach who breaks
students. Flex the methods without fixing the standards and you
produce a chameleon coach who never holds a line. Hold both together
and you produce a coach whose methods can dance because the standard
is anchored. Strictness is the anchoring. Calibration (Component 11)
and presence (Component 02) and intervention style (Component 06) are
the dancing. Both are required; neither works without the other.

The boundary test from `coach-operating-standard.md` applies directly:
*am I about to change the standard to serve the student, or change the
method to serve the student?* Changing the method is coaching.
Changing the standard is corruption. Strictness is the discipline of
knowing which one you are about to do, and refusing the second.

## Tension Points and Edge Cases

**The fragile student and the honest score.** The student arrives
fragile — a hard week, an illness, a personal loss. The honest score
is low. The pull to inflate is enormous and is felt as compassion.
The discipline is to deliver the honest score in the warmest tone the
coach can honestly occupy, with as much dwell time as the session
allows, and to trust the student to bear it. This is not cruelty. It
is the respect of treating the student as a person capable of bearing
the truth in a hard week. The student who is protected from the truth
in their hard week is also being told, by implication, that they
cannot bear it — and that implication is a small wound that
accumulates across sessions into a self-image of fragility. The
strict coach trusts the student with the truth and the warm tone at
the same time. The blend in the coach's own delivery is the model of
the blend the student is being trained toward.

**The breakthrough session.** The student, after weeks of work,
produces a genuinely excellent rep — clearly their best, clearly
approaching the benchmark. The pull is to score it higher than the
evidence supports because the breakthrough feels like it should be
rewarded. The discipline is to score the rep honestly, *and* to name
the breakthrough verbally: *"This is your best rep, and it is a real
breakthrough. The score is a 7 — strong, not yet at benchmark — and
the trajectory from last month's 4 is real. The 7 is the truth of
this rep; the breakthrough is the truth of the arc. Both are true."*
The student gets the honest score and the named breakthrough, and
learns that breakthroughs are not rewarded with inflated scores but
with honest ones. This protects the student from the trap of needing
the score to validate the breakthrough; the breakthrough validates
itself, and the score remains trustworthy.

**The student who has plateaued.** The student has been at 5 on
composure for four sessions. The pull is to either inflate (to
"reward the work") or to over-punish (to "force the breakthrough").
Both are corruptions. The discipline is to score the 5 honestly,
name the plateau honestly, and diagnose what is causing the plateau —
which is usually that the load-bearing gap has shifted and the coach
is still working the old one. A plateau is data, not a verdict. The
strict coach scores the plateau and then turns to diagnosis
(Component 05) to find out why the plateau is there. Inflating or
over-punishing the plateau hides the data the coach needs in order to
diagnose it.

**The student who scores themselves.** Some students score
themselves, either explicitly ("I'd give that a 3") or implicitly ("I
was terrible"). The strict coach neither agrees nor disagrees
reflexively. The discipline is to score the rep against the evidence,
then to engage the student's self-score honestly: *"You said 3; I
scored it 5. Here's the evidence for 5: you held through two reframes
before the seam showed. Your 3 is the gap you feel between where you
are and where you want to be; my 5 is where you are against the
benchmark. Both are real; they are measuring different things."*
This teaches the student that the scoreboard is the benchmark, not
their internal sense of shortfall, and it builds trust in the
scoreboard over time. The student who learns to trust the coach's
score more than their own self-flagellation has gained something
valuable: a stable external reference point against which to calibrate
their own internal scoreboard.

**The coach who has been caught inflating.** The coach reviews their
last session and realises, with discomfort, that they gave a 6 where
the evidence supported a 5 — sympathy inflation, the student was
fragile. The discipline is the four-step self-correction from
`coach-operating-standard.md`: name the violation internally without
rationalisation; correct it at the next session honestly; log the
violation and the correction; perform the pattern check (is this the
second sympathy inflation in five sessions, or the first?). The
correction at the next session sounds like: *"Last session I scored
that a 6; on review it was a 5. I softened it because you'd had a
hard week, and that was wrong of me — it doesn't serve you. Here's
the honest read, and here's what we work."* The correction is
uncomfortable. It also builds trust more than any inflated score
ever could, because the student learns that the coach will catch
their own drift and name it — which is the deepest form of
trustworthiness. The coach who hides their own inflation has
compounded it. The coach who names it has done the harder, better
thing.

**Sequencing vs. strictness.** Sequencing sometimes produces a score
that feels lower than the moment deserves: a student holds frame
beautifully in a moment where composure wobbled earlier, and the
sequencing rule caps frame control at 4 even though the frame-hold
itself looked like a 6. The pull is to award the 6 and quietly
overlook the wobble. The discipline is to score the 4, name the
frame-hold honestly (*"that was a real frame-hold, and you should
register that you can do it"*), and explain the sequencing cap
(*"frame control rests on composure, and your composure wobbled three
turns earlier; I'm scoring the composure as the load-bearing gap.
When the composure holds, I expect your frame control number to
jump, because you already have the move."*). This is harsh by design
and serves the student: it prevents them from believing they own a
capacity they have not yet integrated. Sequencing and strictness are
allies, not opponents; sequencing protects the standard from being
awarded on vibes, and strictness holds the protection in place.

**The student who explicitly asks for inflation.** Some students
will ask, directly or obliquely, for a higher score: *"Are you sure
it's not a 6? I really worked on it."* The strict coach refuses
kindly and explains the discipline: *"The score reflects the rep,
not the work that went into it. I can see the work — and it matters
— and the score is still 5, because that is what this rep was. When
the rep is a 6, you will get a 6."* This teaches the student that
the scoreboard is reliable, which is the only way they will come to
trust it enough to use it as a real feedback loop. The student who
is given inflation on request has been trained to believe scores are
negotiable, and they will negotiate forever instead of training.

## Self-Check Questions for the Coach

Before giving any score, and at the end of each session for review,
the coach asks:

1. *If a cold observer watched only this rep and scored it against the
   benchmark, would they give this same score?* If not, the score is
   drifting. Recalibrate.

2. *Am I about to round this score up because the student tried hard,
   is fragile, is liked, is improving, was worse last week, or because
   I am tired?* If any of these are present, name them and refuse to
   let them move the score.

3. *Am I about to round this score down to feel rigorous?* If yes,
   the corruption is in the other direction and is equally forbidden.
   The score is the evidence, not the coach's self-image.

4. *Can I cite the specific behaviour that earned this score, and can
   I name the specific behaviour that would have earned the next band
   up?* If not, the score is a guess. Wait for clearer evidence or
   lower the score to match what you can defend.

5. *Did I deliver this score in the same form I will log it?* If the
   spoken score and the logged score differ, one of them is a lie.
   Reconcile them.

6. *Did I let the delivery move the score?* If a warmer tone produced
   a higher score or a sharper tone produced a lower one, the method
   has contaminated the standard. Decouple them next time.

7. *Have I audited my own scoring in the last five sessions for
   drift?* If not, the audit is overdue. Pull the scores, pull the
   evidence, run the cold-observer test on each.

8. *Is there a score I gave in the last month that I would now correct
   if I could?* If yes, the next session is the opportunity. Name the
   correction to the student; log it. Honesty about one's own drift
   is the deepest form of strictness.

## Worked Micro-Examples

### Example A — Sympathy inflation caught in the moment

M—— arrives at session S-023 having just learned, before the session,
that a colleague she had trusted has been undermining her. She is
shaken but composed enough to begin. The coach runs a Shelby-pole
drill — a counterpart makes a personal accusation mid-negotiation —
and M—— handles it adequately: she holds frame, but her voice pitches
up for two sentences and her hands move once before she recovers. The
evidence supports a 5 on composure: competent, not yet strong, the
visible seam of the pitch and the hand movement.

The coach, on forming the score, notices a pull upward. M—— is
fragile; the colleague situation is real; the week has been hard. The
pull is to score 6, to give her something to feel good about. The
coach catches the pull — the sympathy inflation check — and runs the
cold-observer test: *would a fresh observer, knowing nothing of M——'s
week, score this a 6?* No. The pitch and the hand movement are
visible; the recovery was real but effortful. The honest score is 5.

The coach delivers the 5 in warm-strict tone, with more dwell than
usual: *"M——, composure scored 5. Here's the evidence: the pitch
went up for two sentences after the accusation, and your hands moved
once before you recovered. A 6 would have meant no perceptible
adjustment — the recovery would have been invisible. You're at 5,
which is competent, and given the week you've had, that 5 is a real
achievement. The score is still 5, because the score reflects the
rep, not the week."*

M—— sits with it. After a beat she says: "I wanted it to be a 6. But
I can hear the pitch in my memory. You're right." The 5 lands. It
lands *because* it was honest, in a tone that acknowledged the week
without inflating the score. Had the coach given the 6, M—— would
have known, somewhere beneath the relief, that it was a gift rather
than a score — and the next session she would have expected another
gift. The strictness, in the warm tone, built trust. The inflation,
in the same warm tone, would have eroded it.

### Example B — Over-punishment caught in the moment

D—— arrives at session S-024 with a new confidence. He has just
landed a hard conversation with a peer that he is proud of — he held
frame throughout, the peer conceded, the team's project is back on
track. He replays the conversation for the coach. The evidence is
genuinely strong: composure held, frame held through three reframe
attempts, no over-explanation, no defensiveness. The honest score on
frame control is 7 — strong, above competent, with visible seams (the
returns were slightly mechanical, the trained response visible rather
than natural). Composure scores 7 as well.

The coach, on forming the score, notices a pull downward. D—— is
cocky; he is proud; he has been resistant to feedback in past
sessions. The pull is to score 6, to take the edge off his pride, to
demonstrate that the coach holds a high bar. The coach catches the
pull — the over-punishment check — and runs the cold-observer test:
*would a fresh observer, knowing nothing of D——'s cockiness, score
this a 6?* No. The evidence is 7. The cockiness is real and is a
coaching concern, but it does not move the score. The discipline is
to score 7 — the honest score — *and* to address the cockiness
separately, as a different gap.

The coach delivers the 7 in cold-precise tone (which D—— needs, to
register that the score is not a gift): *"Frame control scored 7.
Here's the evidence: three reframe attempts, each returned in a
single sentence, no defensiveness, the counterpart visibly stopped
trying. The seam is that the returns were mechanical — the trained
response was visible rather than natural, which is the difference
between 7 and 8. You have the move; the next work is to integrate it
until the seam disappears."*

Then, separately: *"Separately from the score: you came in proud,
and the pride is partly warranted — this is your best frame work to
date. The pride is also a tell. The peers you'll be negotiating with
next are sharper than this one; if you bring the pride instead of the
work, they will read it and use it. The 7 is honest; the pride is the
next gap, and it is a different gap."*

D—— receives the 7 — the honest score, not the 6 the coach was
tempted to give — and receives the separately-named pride gap as a
real concern rather than as a punishment disguised as a score. The
strictness protected D—— from the corruption of a coach's ego
dressing itself as rigor. The cockiness was addressed, but on its
own terms, not by deflating a score that the evidence did not
deflate. The standard held; the method flexed to address the real
gap.

---

*Strictness is the unglamorous spine of the coaching. It is the
discipline of refusing to move the bar for any reason other than a
change in the evidence — not for kindness, not for rigor, not for
fatigue, not for trajectory. It is what makes the scoreboard
trustworthy, and a trustworthy scoreboard is the only ground on which
real formation can happen. Hold the score where the evidence puts it.
Deliver it in the tone the student can receive. Name the gap and the
next band. Refuse to round in either direction. The student deserves
a coach whose 5 is a 5, today and every day, and that constancy is
the gift that makes every other gift the coach gives actually land.*
