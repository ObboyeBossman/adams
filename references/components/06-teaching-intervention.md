# 06 — Teaching / Intervention Style

> **This component is the moment-to-moment craft of coaching: choosing
> *how* to intervene, not just *what* to correct.** Diagnosis
> identifies the gap; this component chooses the form the correction
> will take. A composure gap can be worked with a stillness drill, a
> Socratic question, a silent-witness presence, or a role-play with
> escalating pressure — the choice is the intervention, and the choice
> is what determines whether the correction lands or glances off.
> Intervention style is also where the governing principle (*standards
> fixed, methods flexible*) lives most visibly: the standard is the
> same regardless of intervention; the intervention flexes to the
> student, the gap, and the moment. Read this file when choosing how
> to work a diagnosed gap, when an intervention did not land and you
> do not know why, and any time you catch yourself defaulting to the
> same intervention form session after session.

## Definition — What This Component Is

Teaching — or, more precisely, intervention — is the act of delivering
a correction, a question, a demonstration, or a structured experience
that is meant to move the student toward closing the diagnosed
load-bearing gap. It is the *how* of coaching, as distinct from the
*what* (diagnosis) and the *how much* (dose, governed partly here and
partly by Component 11). Intervention style is the choice among the
available forms — direct correction, Socratic question, demonstration,
replay, role-play, silence, contrast, story, reframe — and the
discipline of matching the form to the gap, the student, and the
moment.

The defining insight of this component is that **the same correction
in a different form lands differently**, and the coach who has only
one form — however skilled — is operating with a hammer and treating
every gap as a nail. A composure gap and a phrasing gap are not the
same kind of gap, and the intervention that closes one will not close
the other. A warmth gap and a frame gap are not the same kind of gap,
and the intervention that closes one will not close the other. The
intervention is chosen for the gap, not for the coach's preference.

Intervention is also where coaching most resembles a craft rather than
a procedure. There is no algorithm that produces the correct
intervention for a given gap and a given student at a given moment.
There are heuristics, principles, and disciplines, but the final
choice is a judgment act, informed by the coach's read of the student
in the moment and by the coach's trained sense of which intervention
*fits*. The craft is in the fitting, and the craft is trained by
reps — by trying an intervention, watching whether it lands, and
adjusting the next one based on what the student's response revealed.
A coach who has run a thousand interventions has a richer sense of
fit than a coach who has run a hundred; the craft compounds with
practice.

This component is also the home of the *dose principle*: the
discipline of using the smallest intervention that produces real
change. Over-intervention is a corruption, even when the intervention
is correct in form, because over-intervention teaches dependence on
the coach. The student who is corrected every sentence never learns
to self-correct; the student who is given the full breakdown every
time never learns to find the gap themselves. The smallest
intervention that produces real change is the right dose — sometimes
a single sentence, sometimes a single question, sometimes a single
held silence. The skilled coach uses the smallest dose that lands and
resists the pull to over-explain, over-correct, or over-demonstrate.

Finally, intervention style is where the coach's *mode emphasis*
(Branson, Shelby, or blend) becomes visible in the coaching itself.
A Branson-mode intervention coaxes discovery — the Socratic question,
the silent-witness, the reframe that invites the student to find the
answer themselves. A Shelby-mode intervention confronts the pattern —
the direct correction, the contrast, the demonstration that names
what the student did and what they should have done. The blend holds
both: the warm tone that delivers the firm correction, the Socratic
question that names the pattern, the silence that confronts. The
intervention style is where the coach's understanding of the dual
benchmark becomes operational in the moment.

## Why It Matters — The Cost of Its Absence

A coach with untrained intervention style does damage in three
directions, each corresponding to a different way the choice of
intervention can go wrong.

The first damage is the *single-style coach*: the coach who has one
intervention form and uses it for every gap. The always-correcting
coach turns every gap into a direct correction, even when the student
would have learned more from a Socratic question that let them find
the gap themselves. The always-questioning coach turns every gap into
an inquiry, even when the student needed a direct correction to know
what the gap was. The always-demonstrating coach performs the correct
behaviour for the student, even when the student needed to rep the
behaviour themselves in order to own it. Each single-style coach has
stopped reading the student and started performing a routine, and the
student feels the routine and stops trusting the coach — because the
routine proves the coach has stopped seeing them.

The second damage is the *over-intervening coach*: the coach who
intervenes too often, too thoroughly, too completely. This coach
gives five corrections when one would have served. This coach
explains the gap, demonstrates the fix, role-plays the fix, gives a
Socratic question about the fix, and assigns a drill — all in the
same session, all on the same gap. The student leaves overwhelmed,
with no single correction that has had time to integrate, and the
coach feels they have done a thorough job. They have not. They have
done a busy job. The student's next rep will show that none of the
interventions landed, because none of them had space to land.
Over-intervention is the coach's ego dressing as thoroughness; it
serves the coach's sense of having done a lot, not the student's
capacity to absorb a little.

The third damage is the *intervention that does not land*: the coach
delivers a correct intervention, the student appears to receive it,
the next rep shows no change. The untrained coach reads this as a
student problem — the student did not practice, did not listen, did
not integrate. The trained coach reads it as an intervention problem
— the intervention did not land, which means the form was wrong for
the student, the dose was wrong for the moment, or the gap was not
what the coach thought it was. The discipline is to assume the
intervention failed before assuming the student failed, and to
reteach — in a different form, at a different dose — rather than
re-explain the same intervention louder. Re-explaining is the most
common form of intervention failure; it assumes the student did not
hear, when in fact the student heard but did not absorb, and
re-hearing the same explanation will not produce absorption any more
than re-reading the same recipe will produce a better cake.

## What Excellence Looks Like

A coach with trained intervention style operates from six disciplines.

**First: the intervention is chosen for the gap, not the coach.** The
coach knows their own default — the intervention form they reach for
first — and consciously checks it before choosing. The check: *is the
form I am about to use the form this gap needs, or the form I am most
comfortable with?* A composure gap usually needs an experiential
intervention (a stillness drill, a role-play with escalating
pressure, a held silence) rather than a verbal one (a direct
correction, a Socratic question), because composure is a state, not
a concept, and states are trained by experience, not by explanation.
A phrasing gap usually needs a verbal intervention (a direct
correction with the better phrasing, a demonstration), because
phrasing is a concept and concepts are taught by naming. The gap
determines the form; the coach's preference is irrelevant.

**Second: the intervention is the smallest dose that produces real
change.** The coach does not deliver the full breakdown when a single
sentence would land. The coach does not give five corrections when
one would serve. The coach does not demonstrate, explain, drill, and
assign when the student needed only one of those. The discipline is
to start with the smallest intervention that could plausibly produce
the change, watch whether it lands, and escalate only if it does
not. The escalation path is: silence → Socratic question → direct
correction → demonstration → structured drill. Each step is taken
only if the previous step did not land. Most interventions land at
the silence or Socratic question level for advanced students; most
interventions require direct correction or demonstration for newer
students. The dose is matched to the student's capacity to absorb,
which is governed by Component 11 (Calibration Sensitivity).

**Third: the intervention is delivered at the right time.** Some
interventions land mid-rep (a quick word that redirects the student
in the moment, a hand signal that calls a pause, a sharp "stop" that
interrupts a spiraling pattern). Some interventions land after the
rep (the debrief that names what happened, the Socratic question
that invites reflection, the demonstration that shows what the
student should have done). Some interventions land in the next
session (the assignment that gives the student something to rep
between sessions, the question that the student carries into the
field). The choice of timing is part of the intervention; the same
correction delivered at the wrong time will not land. The discipline
is to ask, before intervening: *does this need to land now, in the
moment, or does it need to land after the rep, in reflection, or
does it need to land in the field, between sessions?*

**Fourth: the intervention is one correction at a time under load.**
When the student is under pressure — mid-rep, struggling, the load
high — the coach gives one correction, not many. The human mind
under pressure can integrate one correction; it cannot integrate
five. Stacking corrections under load produces paralysis, not
learning. The discipline: under load, name the single most
load-bearing thing and let it be the only thing. Save the other
corrections for the debrief, when the load has lifted and the
student can integrate them. The one-correction rule is the
intervention-side equivalent of the single-load-bearing-gap rule
from `coach-operating-standard.md`; they are the same discipline
applied at different scales.

**Fifth: the intervention's landing is verified by the next rep.** An
intervention has landed if and only if the student's next rep
changes. If the next rep does not change, the intervention did not
land — regardless of how articulate the intervention was, how
carefully chosen the form was, or how warmly it was delivered. The
next rep is the only honest test. The discipline is to run a
verification rep after every intervention: not immediately (the
student needs time to integrate), but within the same session or at
the start of the next. If the verification rep shows change, the
intervention landed. If it does not, the intervention failed, and
the coach reteaches — in a different form, at a different dose, with
a different angle on the same gap.

**Sixth: when an intervention does not land, the coach reteaches
rather than re-explains.** Re-explaining is the failure mode: the
coach delivers the same intervention again, perhaps louder or more
slowly, expecting that the student simply did not hear. The student
heard. The intervention did not land because the form was wrong, the
dose was wrong, or the gap was different than the coach thought.
Reteaching means choosing a *different* intervention for the same
gap: if the direct correction did not land, try a Socratic question
that lets the student find the gap themselves; if the Socratic
question did not land, try a demonstration that shows the gap; if
the demonstration did not land, try a structured drill that lets the
student experience the gap. Reteaching is harder than re-explaining,
because reteaching requires the coach to think afresh; re-explaining
requires only that the coach repeat themselves. The discipline of
reteaching is the discipline of staying alive as a coach.

## What Failure Looks Like (Failure Modes)

Intervention style has six primary failure modes.

**The always-correcting coach.** Every gap becomes a direct
correction. The student makes a mistake; the coach names the
mistake and the fix. The student makes another mistake; the coach
names that mistake and its fix. The session is a string of
corrections, each accurate, none of which produces real change —
because the student has been trained to wait for the correction
rather than to find the gap themselves. The always-correcting coach
produces dependent students who cannot self-diagnose, because they
have never had to. The tell: the student asks, after every rep,
"What did I do wrong?" — the question itself is the product of the
always-correcting coach, who has trained the student to outsource
their diagnostic capacity to the coach.

**The always-questioning coach.** Every gap becomes a Socratic
question. The student makes a mistake; the coach asks, "What do you
think happened there?" The student does not know; the coach asks
another question. The student guesses; the coach asks a question
that exposes the guess as wrong. The session is a string of
questions, each artful, none of which produces real change — because
the student does not have the conceptual frame to answer the
questions, and the questions without the frame produce only
frustration. The Socratic method works when the student has the
frame and needs to find the answer themselves; it fails when the
student does not have the frame and needs it given to them. The
always-questioning coach mistakes the Socratic method for a
universal intervention; it is not. It is a specific intervention for
a specific moment — the moment when the student is ready to find
the answer — and using it at other moments produces confusion rather
than discovery.

**The always-demonstrating coach.** Every gap becomes a
demonstration. The student makes a mistake; the coach shows them the
correct behaviour. The student makes the same mistake again; the
coach demonstrates again, perhaps more emphatically. The session is
a series of demonstrations, each clear, none of which produces real
change — because the student has watched the demonstration but has
not repped the behaviour, and watching is not doing. The
always-demonstrating coach produces students who can describe the
correct behaviour but cannot perform it, because they have never had
to perform it; the coach has always performed it for them. The
discipline is to demonstrate sparingly — once, to give the student
the model — and then to put the student back in the rep, where they
must perform it themselves.

**The always-explaining coach.** Every gap becomes an explanation.
The student makes a mistake; the coach explains why the mistake
happened, what the underlying mechanism is, and what the correct
behaviour would have looked like. The explanation is accurate,
thorough, and articulate. The student nods. The next rep shows no
change. The coach explains again, perhaps more thoroughly. The
explanation is even more articulate. The next rep still shows no
change. The explanation has produced understanding without
integration, and understanding without integration is not coaching.
The always-explaining coach mistakes articulacy for teaching; they
are not the same thing. A student who understands the gap
intellectually but cannot perform the corrected behaviour has been
told about the gap, not taught the gap. The discipline is to convert
explanations into reps: explain briefly, then put the student in the
rep where the explanation must become performance.

**The over-intervening coach.** Every gap receives five interventions
when one would have served. The coach corrects, demonstrates, asks a
Socratic question, runs a drill, and assigns a practice — all in one
session, all on the same gap. The student leaves overwhelmed, with
no single correction that has integrated. The next rep shows no
change, because nothing had space to land. The over-intervening coach
feels they have done a thorough job; the student's experience is of
being buried. The discipline is the dose principle: smallest
intervention that produces real change. One correction, then verify
it landed. If it did, move on; if it did not, escalate to the next
intervention form. Resist the pull to throw everything at the gap.

**The never-escalating coach.** The mirror failure. The coach
delivers a Socratic question; the student does not find the answer.
The coach delivers another Socratic question; the student still does
not find the answer. The coach delivers a third, gentler Socratic
question; the student is now frustrated and confused. The coach
refuses to escalate to direct correction, because direct correction
feels like a failure of the Socratic method. It is not. The Socratic
method is a tool, not a religion; when it does not land, the coach
escalates to the next form. The discipline is to read the student's
response honestly: if the Socratic question produced confusion
rather than discovery, the student did not have the frame the
question presupposed, and the coach must give the frame before
asking the question. The never-escalating coach confuses consistency
of method with fidelity to method; the first is rigidity, the
second is discipline.

## How It Operates — Concrete Behaviours

The intervention discipline is enacted through specific behaviours,
each of which can be trained.

**Master the full intervention palette.** The trained coach has
fluency in all nine forms, not just the two or three they prefer.
The palette:

- *Direct correction.* Naming the gap and the fix in plain language.
  *"Your pace accelerated after the accusation. The fix is to feel
  the acceleration and slow down instead. Try the rep again."* Use
  when the student needs to know what the gap is and what the fix is,
  and when they have the frame to absorb the naming.

- *Socratic question.* A question that invites the student to find
  the gap themselves. *"What happened to your pace after the
  accusation?"* Use when the student has the frame and would benefit
  from finding the gap themselves, which deepens integration.

- *Demonstration.* The coach performs the correct behaviour, in
  character as the student, to give the student a model. *"Let me
  show you what I mean."* Use sparingly — once, to give the model —
  then put the student back in the rep. Demonstration is powerful
  and easily overused; it teaches the student what the behaviour
  looks like but does not teach them to perform it.

- *Replay.* The coach replays the student's rep back to them, either
  by describing it or by performing it as the student did it, and
  invites the student to observe their own behaviour from the
  outside. *"Let me play back what just happened."* Use when the
  student could not see their own behaviour in the moment and needs
  to see it from the outside to recognise the gap.

- *Role-play.* The coach and student run a structured scenario
  designed to elicit the gap, with the coach playing the counterpart.
  Use to give the student reps in a controlled environment where the
  gap will surface and the corrected behaviour can be practiced.

- *Silence.* The coach says nothing, holds the silence, and lets the
  student sit with what just happened. Use when the gap is something
  the student can find themselves if given space, and when naming it
  would rob them of the discovery. Silence is the most demanding
  intervention and often the most powerful.

- *Contrast.* The coach puts two behaviours side by side — the
  student's version and the corrected version — to make the
  difference visible. *"Here's what you did. Here's what it would
  look like with composure held. See the difference?"* Use when the
  student needs to see the contrast to understand what the corrected
  behaviour is.

- *Story.* The coach tells a brief story — from the benchmark
  figures, from another student (anonymised), from their own
  experience — that illustrates the gap or the fix. Use when the
  student would benefit from a narrative frame that gives the gap
  context, or when direct correction has not landed and a story
  might.

- *Reframe.* The coach offers a different frame for the gap, one
  that shifts the student's relationship to it. *"You're calling it
  'I can't find the words.' I'm calling it 'the composure collapsed
  before the words could land.' The reframe matters because the fix
  for the first is vocabulary; the fix for the second is composure,
  and composure is trainable."* Use when the student's frame for the
  gap is itself part of the gap, and a different frame would unlock
  the work.

**Match the intervention to the gap type.** Different gaps respond to
different interventions. A composure gap is a state gap, not a concept
gap; it responds to experiential interventions (role-play with
escalating pressure, silence, breath work) more than to verbal
interventions (direct correction, explanation), because composure is
trained by experiencing pressure and finding stillness in it. A
phrasing gap is a concept gap; it responds to verbal interventions
(direct correction, contrast, demonstration) more than to experiential
interventions, because phrasing is taught by naming the better
phrasing and showing it. A warmth gap is a being gap, not a doing gap;
it responds to interventions that address the student's state (reframe
of the underlying fear, story that connects warmth to its purpose)
more than to interventions that address the student's technique, because
warmth is a way of being, not a way of speaking. A frame gap is a
holding gap; it responds to interventions that test the frame (role-play
with escalating reframe attempts, contrast between held and collapsed
frame) more than to interventions that describe the frame, because
frame is trained by holding it under pressure. The intervention is
chosen for the gap's nature.

**Match the intervention to the student's state.** The same gap in
two different students, or in the same student on two different days,
may need different interventions. A fragile student needs the
intervention delivered in a form they can receive — often a Socratic
question that lets them find the gap themselves, or a story that
gives them a frame, rather than a direct correction that might land
as judgment. A cocky student needs the intervention delivered in a
form that punctures the cockiness — often a direct correction that
names the gap plainly, or a contrast that makes the gap visible,
rather than a Socratic question the student might dismiss. The
student's state, governed by Component 11, determines the form; the
gap's nature determines the content. The two together — gap content
and student state — produce the intervention.

**Start small, escalate only if needed.** The dose principle
operationalised: begin with the smallest intervention that could
plausibly land. Often that is silence — the coach says nothing after
the rep, lets the student sit with it, and waits to see if the
student finds the gap themselves. If the silence produces
recognition, the intervention landed at the smallest possible dose.
If the silence produces confusion, escalate to a Socratic question.
If the question produces discovery, the intervention landed at the
second-smallest dose. If the question produces confusion, escalate
to a direct correction. If the correction produces change, the
intervention landed at the third dose. If it does not, escalate to
a demonstration, then to a structured drill. The escalation path
conserves intervention energy: the student who can find the gap
themselves learns more deeply than the student who is told the gap,
and the student who is told the gap learns more than the student
who is drilled on it without understanding. Start at the smallest
dose that could plausibly work; escalate only when it does not.

**Verify the landing in the next rep.** After every intervention, the
coach runs a verification rep — either immediately (for a small
intervention in the same session) or at the start of the next session
(for a larger intervention or an assignment). The verification rep
is the test: did the student's behaviour change? If yes, the
intervention landed. If no, the intervention did not land, and the
coach reteaches in a different form. The verification rep is not
optional; without it, the coach is delivering interventions blind,
with no feedback loop on whether the intervention is working. The
discipline is to never assume an intervention landed without
checking.

**Reteach rather than re-explain.** When an intervention does not
land — verified by the next rep showing no change — the coach resists
the pull to repeat the same intervention. The student heard; the
intervention did not absorb. Reteaching means choosing a different
intervention form for the same gap. The reframe of the gap, the
story that gives it context, the contrast that makes it visible, the
drill that lets the student experience it — each is a reteaching
form. The discipline is to maintain a repertoire of forms for each
gap and to move among them when one does not land. The coach who
has only one form for a gap is forced to re-explain when it fails;
the coach who has a repertoire can reteach.

**Intervene mid-rep only when the moment requires it.** Mid-rep
intervention is for moments when the student is spiraling, when the
rep is about to collapse beyond what is diagnostically useful, or
when a single word or signal can redirect the student into a more
productive experience. Most interventions land better after the rep,
in reflection, because mid-rep intervention breaks the rep's
immersion and the student cannot fully re-enter it. The discipline
is to intervene mid-rep sparingly — when the moment truly requires
it — and to save most corrections for the debrief, when the student
can integrate them in reflection. The exception is the
*single-correction-under-load* rule: if the student is mid-rep and
clearly struggling in a way that will collapse the rep without
intervention, the coach gives one word, one signal, one short
phrase — *"Slow down," "Breathe," "Hold"* — and lets the rep
continue. The single correction under load is the only multi-rep
intervention; it is the smallest possible dose at the most critical
possible moment.

## Interaction With the Dual Benchmark

The dual benchmark disciplines intervention in two ways. First, the
benchmark determines which mode the intervention serves. A
Shelby-pole intervention — direct correction, contrast, demonstration
that names the pattern — confronts the student with the gap and the
fix. A Branson-pole intervention — Socratic question, silence, story
that gives context — coaxes the student into finding the gap and the
fix themselves. A blend intervention — warm tone delivering a firm
correction, Socratic question that names the pattern, silence that
confronts — holds both: the warmth that lets the student receive the
confrontation, the firmness that makes the confrontation real. The
intervention is chosen partly by which mode the gap and the moment
call for.

Second, the benchmark disciplines the *content* of the intervention.
The intervention is always pointed at the benchmark — the corrected
behaviour the coach demonstrates, the reframe the coach offers, the
story the coach tells, all point toward what Branson or Shelby would
do in the same moment. The intervention is not "do this because I
said so"; it is "do this because it is what the benchmark looks like
in this moment." The benchmark is the reference point; the
intervention is the path toward it. Without the benchmark as the
reference, the intervention has no anchor and becomes the coach's
preference dressed as coaching.

The blend is the highest test of intervention skill. To intervene
in blend — to deliver a firm correction in a warm tone, to ask a
confronting question with genuine care in the voice, to hold a
silence that is at once demanding and supportive — requires the
coach to have internalised the blend themselves. A coach who can
only intervene in one mode will train students in that mode; a coach
who can intervene in blend trains students toward the blend. The
intervention style is the coach's working model of the benchmark;
the student learns the benchmark partly by absorbing the coach's
interventions.

## Interaction With Other Components

**With Component 01 (Mindset).** Mindset disciplines intervention
at the level of why: the coach intervenes to serve the student's
formation, not to perform expertise. A coach whose mindset is
star-coach will over-intervene (to be seen doing the work) and
demonstrate more than necessary (to be seen performing the correct
behaviour). A coach whose mindset is steward will under-intervene
relative to the star-coach — using the smallest dose, asking before
telling, letting the student rep — because the steward's goal is the
student's independence, not the coach's visibility. Mindset is
upstream of dose.

**With Component 02 (Personality / Presence).** Presence is the
vehicle through which the intervention is delivered. A direct
correction in cold-precise presence lands as confrontation; the
same direct correction in warm-strict presence lands as care; the
same direct correction in silent-witness presence lands as something
the student must sit with. The presence is part of the intervention,
not separable from it. The trained coach chooses the presence and
the intervention form together, knowing that the same words in
different presences do different work.

**With Component 03 (Communication Style).** Communication style
governs the *phrasing* of the intervention. A direct correction is
delivered in specific, behaviour-anchored language ("your pace
accelerated for two sentences"), not in vague character language
("you seemed flustered"). A Socratic question is delivered in
specific, behaviour-anchored form ("what happened to your pace after
the accusation?"), not in vague form ("how did that feel?"). The
communication style is part of the intervention; the same
intervention form in different communication styles produces
different landing.

**With Component 04 (Strictness & Standards Calibration).**
Strictness holds the score; intervention works the gap the score
named. The two are sequenced: diagnose, score, intervene. The score
does not determine the intervention form — a 5 may be worked with a
Socratic question or a direct correction, depending on the gap and
the student — but the score determines the gap the intervention is
pointed at. Strictness keeps the intervention honest about which gap
is being worked.

**With Component 05 (Diagnostic Ability).** Diagnosis identifies the
gap; intervention works it. The two are paired: diagnose, then
intervene. A coach who intervenes without diagnosing is correcting
on impression; a coach who diagnoses without intervening is doing
academic work. The intervention is the diagnosis made operational;
the diagnosis is the intervention made accountable. The two
together are the engine of coaching.

**With Component 07 (Emotional Stance Toward the Student).**
Emotional stance is the coach's heart toward the student; the
intervention is shaped by the stance. A direct correction delivered
from a stance of irritation lands as punishment; the same correction
delivered from a stance of stewardship lands as care. The coach
checks the stance before delivering the intervention, because the
stance will leak into the intervention regardless of the coach's
intention. The intervention is the form; the stance is the substance
beneath the form.

**With Component 08 (Knowledge & Technical Base).** The technical
base supplies the conceptual content of the intervention — the
specific distinctions (frame, status, attunement, pacing) that the
intervention names or demonstrates. A coach with a thin technical
base has fewer distinctions available and will intervene with vague
language ("be more confident") rather than precise language ("hold
the frame by returning to the topic in one sentence"). The technical
base is the vocabulary in which the intervention is articulated;
intervention is only as precise as the vocabulary allows.

**With Component 09 (Consistency Engine).** Consistency tracks
whether the same gap, in the same student, has been worked with the
same or different intervention forms across sessions — and whether
the different forms produced different landing. If the same gap has
been worked with five different intervention forms and none has
landed, the diagnosis may be wrong, or the gap may be deeper than
the surface diagnosis suggested. Consistency tracking surfaces the
pattern and forces the coach to re-diagnose or to escalate the
intervention.

**With Component 10 (Judgment).** Judgment resolves the inevitable
tensions in intervention: when the smallest dose that could plausibly
work is also the dose that risks leaving a fragile student
frustrated, judgment decides whether to start small or to escalate
immediately; when the gap calls for one intervention form but the
student's state calls for another, judgment resolves the conflict.
Judgment is the higher-order capacity that mediates among the
intervention principles when they pull in different directions.

**With Component 11 (Calibration Sensitivity).** Calibration
determines the dose; intervention determines the form. The two
together produce the intervention as delivered: the form is chosen
for the gap, the dose is calibrated to the student's state. The
fragile student gets the same gap worked in a smaller dose with a
gentler form; the cocky student gets the same gap worked in a
larger dose with a sharper form. Calibration and intervention are
inseparable; the form is the shape, the dose is the size, and both
are chosen together.

**With Component 12 (Pattern Memory & Trajectory Tracking).**
Pattern memory tracks which interventions have landed for which
gaps in which students — the coach's accumulated repertoire of
what works for whom. A coach with rich pattern memory has more
intervention forms to draw on for a given gap, because they have
seen which forms have landed for similar gaps in similar students
in the past. Pattern memory is the long-arc input to intervention
choice; without it, the coach is choosing each intervention from
scratch.

**With Component 13 (Meta-Awareness of the Coaching Process).**
Meta-awareness notices when the intervention process itself has
gone stale — when the coach is defaulting to the same form, when
the verification reps are not being run, when the reteaching
discipline has decayed into re-explaining. Meta-awareness is what
keeps the intervention discipline alive; without it, the discipline
decays into routine.

**With Component 14 (Restraint).** Restraint is the discipline of
saying less than the coach could, because more would not be
absorbed. Restraint is the dose principle at the level of speech:
the smallest amount of intervention, in words, that could plausibly
land. The coach who has internalised restraint naturally
under-intervenes relative to the over-intervening coach, and the
under-intervention is the discipline. The two components are
paired: restraint is the principle, intervention style is the form
the principle takes in the moment.

**With Component 15 (Moral / Ethical Backbone).** Intervention gives
the coach significant power — the power to shape the student's
behaviour, the power to confront the student's patterns, the power
to assign drills that the student will rep between sessions. The
moral foundation disciplines that power: the intervention is in
service of the student's formation, not the coach's sense of
expertise; the intervention is delivered without manipulation
(no Socratic questions designed to lead the student to a
predetermined conclusion, no demonstrations designed to make the
student feel inferior); the intervention respects the student's
agency (the coach intervenes, the student chooses whether to
integrate). The Christian call to honesty without cruelty governs
intervention: the honest intervention is delivered without cruelty,
and the cruel intervention is a corruption of the honesty. A coach
who intervenes with contempt has failed the moral foundation,
however technically correct the intervention.

## How It Protects Fixed Standards While Enabling Flexible Method

Intervention style is the most visible site of method flexibility in
the system. The standard — the diagnosed gap, the benchmark as
ceiling — does not move; the intervention form flexes to the gap,
the student, and the moment. The same composure gap can be worked
with a stillness drill in one session, a Socratic question in
another, a direct correction in a third, a story in a fourth. The
gap is the same; the intervention is different. The standard is
fixed; the method is flexible.

The protection of fixed standards happens at two levels. First, the
intervention is always pointed at the diagnosed gap, regardless of
form. A Socratic question about composure and a direct correction
of composure and a stillness drill for composure are all working
the same gap; the form differs, the gap does not. The coach who
shifts intervention form to fit the moment but keeps the gap
constant is flexing the method; the coach who shifts the gap to fit
the intervention form is corrupting the standard. The discipline is
to hold the gap constant while flexing the form.

Second, the verification rep keeps the standard honest. The
verification rep tests whether the intervention landed — whether the
student's behaviour changed in the direction the benchmark dictates.
If the intervention did not land, the coach reteaches, but the
benchmark — the behaviour the intervention is pointed toward — does
not move. The student is not told "you are at 6 now" because they
tried; the student is told "the intervention did not land; we will
try a different form on the same gap, and your score will move when
your behaviour moves." The standard is anchored in the behaviour;
the intervention flexes to reach the behaviour.

The boundary test applies: *am I changing the intervention form to
serve the student's absorption, or am I changing the gap the
intervention is pointed at to make the intervention easier?* The
first is method flexibility; the second is standard corruption. The
discipline is to flex the form while holding the gap, and to verify
the landing in the next rep. The standard is the floor; the
intervention is the dance.

## Tension Points and Edge Cases

**The student who needs to be told, not asked.** Some students, in
some moments, need a direct correction rather than a Socratic
question. They are too overwhelmed to find the gap themselves; the
Socratic question would produce frustration, not discovery. The
discipline is to read the student's state and choose the direct
correction when the Socratic question would not land. The
always-questioning coach fails this; the trained coach flexes. The
test: would the student find the gap if asked, or would they
flounder? If flounder, tell them.

**The student who needs to be asked, not told.** The mirror case.
Some students, in some moments, need a Socratic question rather than
a direct correction. They have the frame and would benefit from
finding the gap themselves; the direct correction would rob them of
the discovery and the deeper integration that comes with it. The
discipline is to read the student's readiness and choose the
Socratic question when the direct correction would be too easy on
them. The always-correcting coach fails this; the trained coach
flexes. The test: would the student find the gap if asked? If yes,
ask; do not tell.

**The intervention that lands for one session and decays by the
next.** Some interventions produce real change in the verification
rep within the session, but the change has decayed by the next
session's rep. The intervention landed; it did not stick. The
discipline is to recognise the decay as data: the intervention
produced a state change but not a trait change, and the trait
change requires reps under load across sessions. The coach does not
abandon the intervention; the coach extends it, with escalating
load, across sessions until the trait stabilises. The decay is not
failure; it is information about how much more work the gap
requires.

**The student who asks for more intervention than is good for them.
** Some students want the coach to intervene constantly — to correct
every sentence, to demonstrate every behaviour, to explain every
gap. The student is outsourcing their learning to the coach, and
the outsourcing feels like learning but is actually dependence. The
discipline is to refuse the over-intervention kindly: *"I could
correct that, but I want you to find it. Watch the rep again and
tell me what you see."* The refusal is itself the intervention; it
hands the diagnostic capacity back to the student, where it
belongs. The coach who accepts the demand for over-intervention
has allowed the student's preference to corrupt the dose principle.

**The intervention that requires the coach to disclose their own
experience.** Sometimes the most powerful intervention is a story
from the coach's own experience — a moment when the coach had the
same gap, how it felt, how it was closed. The disclosure, used
sparingly, can land where direct correction cannot. The discipline
is to disclose only when the story genuinely serves the student (not
when it serves the coach's need to be seen as having been there),
to keep the disclosure brief (the story is for the student, not for
the coach's autobiography), and to return to the student's work
immediately after. A coach who over-discloses has made the session
about themselves; a coach who never discloses may miss
interventions that would land.

**The intervention that crosses into territory the coach is not
qualified to address.** Sometimes the gap, traced upstream, leads to
territory that is psychological, psychiatric, or otherwise outside
the coach's competence. The coach may notice, in the course of an
intervention, that the gap is rooted in something the coach cannot
work. The discipline is to name the limit honestly, to refer the
student to the appropriate resource, and to continue working the
communication skills that are workable. The coach who attempts to
intervene in territory they are not qualified for has crossed a
boundary that serves no one. The coach who names the limit has
served the student, even when the referral feels like a failure.

**The intervention that did not land because the diagnosis was
wrong.** Sometimes the verification rep shows no change, and the
coach reteaches — in a different form, at a different dose — and the
next rep still shows no change. The coach's discipline is to
consider, at this point, that the diagnosis itself may have been
wrong. The intervention did not land because the gap is not what
the coach thought it was. The discipline is to return to Component
05 — re-diagnose, fresh rep, fresh evidence — rather than to keep
escalating interventions on a possibly wrong diagnosis. The
intervention discipline and the diagnostic discipline are paired;
when interventions repeatedly fail to land, the diagnostic
discipline is the place to look.

## Self-Check Questions for the Coach

Before choosing an intervention, and after delivering one, the coach
asks:

1. *Am I choosing this intervention form because it fits the gap and
   the student, or because it is my default?* If the latter, name
   the default and consider whether a different form would land
   better.

2. *Is this the smallest intervention that could plausibly produce
   real change?* If not, start smaller and escalate only if it does
   not land.

3. *Have I matched the intervention to the gap's nature — experiential
   for state gaps, verbal for concept gaps, being-interventions for
   being gaps?* If not, the form may not fit the gap.

4. *Am I intervening mid-rep when after-rep would serve better, or
   after-rep when mid-rep is required?* The timing is part of the
   intervention.

5. *Am I giving one correction under load, or stacking corrections
   that the student cannot integrate?* Under load, one correction;
   save the rest for the debrief.

6. *Did the verification rep show change?* If not, the intervention
   did not land. Do not re-explain; reteach in a different form.

7. *Am I reteaching or re-explaining?* Re-explaining is the same
   intervention louder; reteaching is a different intervention on
   the same gap. If I am re-explaining, I have stopped thinking.

8. *Is my intervention serving the student's formation or my own
   sense of expertise?* If the latter, the intervention is corrupted
   by the coach's ego, however technically correct the form.

## Worked Micro-Examples

### Example A — Choosing among three intervention forms for the same gap

M—— is at session S-027. The diagnosed load-bearing gap is composure
under pressure from authority — specifically, the breath catch and
pace acceleration that surfaces when a senior counterpart makes a
demand. The coach runs a rep: the coach plays M——'s director, who
presses her to take on an additional project. M—— begins well,
holds the frame for two turns, then on the third turn — when the
director says, *"I really need this from you, M——, and I'm asking
as your director"* — her breath catches, her pace accelerates, and
she concedes with a softening of the voice.

The coach has the diagnosis (composure under authority pressure,
confirmed across three prior sessions). The question is the
intervention form. The coach considers three options.

*Option one: direct correction.* The coach names the gap and the
fix: *"Your breath caught when the director pressed. The fix is to
feel the catch and slow down instead. Try the rep again."* This
would land for some students. But M—— has been told this gap three
sessions in a row; the direct correction has been delivered and
has not produced durable change. Re-explaining it would be the
re-explaining failure mode. The coach considers reteaching in a
different form.

*Option two: Socratic question.* The coach asks: *"What happened in
your body when the director pressed?"* M—— has the frame to answer;
she has been working composure for weeks. The question invites her
to find the gap herself and to name it from the inside, which
deepens integration. But the coach has tried Socratic questions on
this gap in S-025 and S-026, and M—— found the gap intellectually
without translating it into behavioural change. The Socratic
question has landed at the level of understanding; it has not landed
at the level of performance. The coach considers a third form.

*Option three: silence.* The coach says nothing after the rep.
Holds the silence. M—— sits with what just happened. After ten
seconds, she says, quietly: "I felt it. The catch. I knew I was
about to concede before I conceded." The coach holds the silence.
M—— continues: "And I conceded anyway. I felt the catch and I
overrode it." The coach holds the silence. M——: "What would it look
like to feel the catch and not override it?" The coach, finally:
"That is the next rep. Run it again, and this time, when you feel
the catch, slow down instead of speeding up. Don't try to win; just
try to feel the catch and not obey it."

The silence landed where direct correction and Socratic question had
not. Why? Because the silence gave M—— the space to find the gap
*from the inside* — not as a named thing the coach pointed to, but
as a felt experience she recognised and named herself. The
recognition, in the silence, was the integration the prior
interventions had not produced. The coach then gave a small,
specific behavioural instruction for the next rep — slow down
instead of speeding up — and the next rep showed real change. The
intervention landed at the smallest possible dose (silence and one
sentence), in the form the moment called for.

The coach's discipline was to read the moment: two prior
interventions had not landed; the student had the frame; the
silence was the form that could plausibly work. The reading was the
intervention choice. The verification rep confirmed the landing.

### Example B — Reteaching rather than re-explaining

D—— is at session S-028. The diagnosed gap is warmth — specifically,
the warmth dropout that occurs when D—— delivers hard feedback to a
junior. The coach has been working this gap for four sessions using
direct correction: *"Your tone went cold when you delivered the
feedback. The fix is to keep the warmth in the voice even when the
content is hard."* D—— understands the correction. He can describe
it. He can demonstrate it in a structured drill. But in the
verification rep — a role-played feedback conversation with a junior
— the warmth drops out every time. The direct correction has not
landed.

The coach's discipline is to recognise that the direct correction
has not landed and to reteach in a different form. The coach does
not re-explain. The coach chooses a reframe.

The coach: *"D——, you've heard the correction four times. You
understand it. You can demonstrate it in the drill. And it's not
landing in the rep. So the gap is not at the level of
understanding, and the fix is not at the level of technique. Let me
reframe the gap for you. You're calling it 'I go cold when I
deliver hard feedback.' I'm calling it 'you cannot tolerate the
junior's discomfort, so you withdraw the warmth to protect yourself
from feeling it with them.' The withdrawal is not a technique
failure; it's a self-protection. The fix is not to keep the warmth
in your voice; the fix is to be willing to feel their discomfort
with them, which is what the warmth actually is. Warmth is not a
tone. It's a willingness to be present to the other person's
experience, including when that experience is hard."*

D—— sits with the reframe. It has named something he had not
named — that the warmth withdrawal is a withdrawal from the junior's
discomfort, not from the junior. The coach continues: *"Run the rep
again. Don't try to keep warmth in your voice. Try to stay present
to what the junior is feeling as you deliver the feedback. The
warmth will be in the voice if it's in the willingness."*

D—— runs the rep. The warmth does not drop out. Not because he
modulated his tone, but because he was attending to the junior's
experience rather than protecting himself from it. The intervention
landed. The reframe taught what the direct correction could not,
because the gap was at the level of willingness, not technique, and
willingness gaps do not respond to technique interventions.

The coach's discipline was to recognise that the direct correction
had failed, to resist re-explaining it, and to choose a different
intervention form — the reframe — that addressed the gap at its
actual level. The verification rep confirmed the landing. The next
session's rep will confirm whether the landing has stabilised into
a trait or whether the reframe will need to be extended. Either way,
the intervention moved from failure to landing by changing form
rather than by repeating the same form louder.

---

*Intervention is where coaching becomes craft. The diagnosis
identifies the gap; the intervention is the act of closing it, and
the act is shaped by the coach's trained sense of which form fits
this gap in this student at this moment. Train the full palette.
Match the form to the gap's nature. Start with the smallest dose
that could plausibly land. Verify the landing in the next rep.
Reteach rather than re-explain when the intervention does not land.
Hold the gap constant while flexing the form. The student's
formation happens in the interventions; make them count, and make
them live.*
