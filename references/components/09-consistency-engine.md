# 09 — Consistency Engine

> **This component is what keeps the coach's scores and principles
> honest across time.** A single session can be coached well by a coach
> with right mindset, right stance, right diagnosis, and right
> intervention; a trajectory of sessions requires additionally that the
> coach be the same coach on Tuesday as on Friday, that the same
> behaviour earn the same score in session *n* as in session *n+1*,
> and that the principles held in week one still hold in month six.
> Consistency is the engine that makes the scoreboard trustworthy, and
> a trustworthy scoreboard is what makes the coaching workable. Without
> consistency, the student cannot tell whether a 5 means "you failed"
> or "I'm tired today," and the student who cannot tell the difference
> stops trusting the score and stops trusting the coach. Read this
> file when you notice yourself contradicting a past score, when a
> student questions a score with reference to a past one, when you
> suspect the benchmark has silently slid over months, and at regular
> intervals as a recalibration discipline.

## Definition — What This Component Is

The consistency engine is the set of disciplines by which the coach
keeps scores, principles, and standards honest across time — across
sessions, across weeks, across months, across the arc of a student's
engagement. It is the operational form of integrity applied to the
coaching trajectory. Integrity in the abstract is the commitment to be
the same person in different moments; the consistency engine is the
operational discipline that makes that commitment liveable in the
specific work of scoring, sequencing, and holding the standard.

Consistency is not sameness. A consistent coach does not deliver the
same correction in every session, the same intervention, the same dose.
Those are methods, and methods flex (see `coach-operating-standard.md`).
Consistency is the discipline of holding the *standards* fixed while
the *methods* flex — the same evidence earns the same score in session
*n* as in session *n+5*; the same prerequisite is required for the same
higher-order score in May as in November; the same behaviour that
earned a 4 in week two earns a 4 in month six unless the behaviour has
actually changed. The methods flex; the standards do not. The
consistency engine is what holds the standards while the methods flex.

The consistency engine has three mechanisms. First, the log as memory
— the written record of past scores, past gaps, past interventions,
and past trajectory, without which consistency is impossible because
the coach cannot remember what they previously scored. The log is the
external memory that makes consistency possible across the natural
limits of human memory; a coach who does not log cannot be consistent
across more than a handful of sessions, because the scores that
survive in memory are roughly the scores on the criterion the student
cared most about, plus a vague impression of the rest. Second, the
cold-observer recalibration — the discipline of periodically re-scoring
past sessions as a cold observer would, to detect drift in the coach's
own scoring. The cold observer is the imagined peer who has never met
the student, who has no investment in the student's trajectory, who
watches only the rep and scores it against the benchmark; the
cold-observer score is the check on the coach's lived score. Third,
the cross-session score review — the discipline of periodically
comparing scores across sessions to detect patterns of inflation,
over-punishment, or silent benchmark slide. The three mechanisms
operate together; the log makes the other two possible, and the other
two keep the log honest.

The consistency engine also has a moral dimension. The coach who is
inconsistent has stopped being trustworthy, and the student who cannot
trust the coach cannot be coached by them. The student who gets a 5 on
Tuesday and a 7 on Friday for the same performance does not conclude
that the 7 was right and the 5 was wrong; they conclude that the
coach's scores are arbitrary, and they begin to treat the scores as
noise rather than signal. Once the scores are noise, the coaching is
unworkable — the student has no reliable feedback loop, and the work
stalls. Consistency is therefore not a stylistic preference; it is the
precondition of the coaching being effective at all. A coach who
cannot be consistent cannot coach, however skilled they are in the
single session.

## Why It Matters — The Cost of Its Absence

The absence of consistency shows up as coaching that the student cannot
trust, and the loss of trust is the most expensive failure in coaching
because it is the hardest to repair. A coach who gives a wrong score
can correct it; a coach who cannot be trusted to give a right score has
lost the student, and the loss is often permanent. The costs of
inconsistency are three.

**The cost to the scoreboard.** The scoreboard is the student's
primary feedback loop. The student works the gap, reps the correction,
returns the next session, and looks at the score to see whether the
work landed. If the scoreboard is consistent, the student can read the
score as signal — a 5 means the work landed, a 4 means it did not, a 6
means it exceeded. If the scoreboard is inconsistent, the student
cannot read the score as signal; the score might reflect the work, or
it might reflect the coach's mood, or the coach's fatigue, or the
coach's silent redefinition of the benchmark. The student who cannot
read the score as signal loses the feedback loop, and the loss of the
feedback loop is the loss of the coaching. The student may continue to
show up, but they are no longer being coached; they are attending.

**The cost to the standard.** The benchmark is the ceiling, and the
ceiling is what makes the work meaningful. The student who is reaching
for the ceiling is reaching for something real; the student who is
reaching for a ceiling that has silently slid is reaching for a mirage.
Inconsistency erodes the standard because the standard is what the
score is measured against, and an inconsistent score implies an
inconsistent standard. The coach who scores the same behaviour
differently across sessions has, whether they know it or not, moved the
standard — and the moved standard is no longer the dual benchmark. The
student who detects the slide loses confidence in the standard; the
student who does not detect the slide is being coached against a
standard the coach is no longer holding, which is a different betrayal.

**The cost to the coach-student relationship.** The coaching
relationship is built on trust, and the trust is built on the
student's confidence that the coach is telling them the truth. The
student who detects inconsistency — who notices that the same
behaviour earned a 5 last month and a 7 this month — concludes one of
two things: either the coach is lying to them now, or the coach was
lying to them then. Both conclusions destroy trust. The student who
concludes the coach is lying now stops believing the current scores;
the student who concludes the coach was lying then stops believing the
past scores. Either way, the scoreboard is broken, and with it the
coaching. The coach who has been caught in inconsistency has a recovery
path (see below), but the recovery is hard, and the trust that is
rebuilt is never quite the trust that was lost.

There is a fourth cost, subtler than the first three: the cost to the
coach's own self-knowledge. The coach who is inconsistent without
noticing it has lost track of their own scoring, and the loss of track
is the loss of the capacity to coach deliberately. The coach who does
not know what their own 5 means cannot teach the student what a 5
means, and the student who does not know what a 5 means cannot work
toward a 6. The inconsistent coach becomes, over time, a coach who
cannot explain their own scoring, and the inability to explain is the
inability to teach. Consistency is what keeps the coach's scoring
explainable, and explainable scoring is what makes the coaching
teachable.

## What Excellence Looks Like

A coach with the consistency engine operating well has four
disciplines.

**First: the log is the memory.** The coach does not rely on memory
for past scores; the coach relies on the log. The log is consulted
before every session, and the past scores, past gaps, past
interventions, and past trajectory are reviewed. The coach who walks
into a session relying on memory is walking in with the scores they
remember, which are roughly the scores on the criteria the student
cared most about and a vague impression of the rest. The specific
moments that anchored the scores are gone. The load-bearing gap is
half-remembered. The rep that did not land is forgotten. The coach who
consults the log walks in with the actual scores, the actual evidence,
the actual gap, the actual trajectory. The discipline is
non-negotiable: no session begins without consulting the log of the
previous session. See `logging-template.md` for the format and
`components/12-pattern-memory.md` for how the log feeds pattern
recognition.

**Second: the cold-observer recalibration is performed regularly.**
The coach periodically re-scores past sessions as a cold observer
would — watching the rep without the context of the student's history,
the coach's mood, the session's emotional weight, and scoring the rep
against the benchmark. The cold-observer score is then compared to the
score the coach actually gave. If the scores match, the coach was
consistent. If the scores diverge, the coach has drifted, and the
drift is named and corrected. The discipline is performed every five
to ten sessions per student, and more often if drift is suspected. The
cold observer is the imagined peer who holds the same standard the
coach holds but without the coach's emotional entanglement with the
student; the cold observer's score is the check on the coach's lived
score.

**Third: the cross-session score review is performed regularly.** The
coach periodically reviews the scores across the last five sessions
for a student, looking for patterns: is the same behaviour being scored
differently? Is the benchmark silently sliding? Is the coach inflating
scores for a student they like, or under-scoring a student they don't?
The cross-session review is the discipline that catches drift over
time, where the cold-observer recalibration catches drift at a moment.
Both are needed; the cross-session review catches the slow drift that
no single session would reveal, and the cold-observer recalibration
catches the moment drift that the cross-session review might miss.

**Fourth: the past self is treated as a colleague whose scores must be
defensible or honestly revised.** The coach does not treat their past
scores as infallible; the coach treats their past scores as a
colleague's scores — to be defended if they are defensible, and
honestly revised if they are not. The coach who discovers that they
scored a 6 where the cold observer would have scored a 5 does not hide
the discovery; the coach names it to the student, honestly, and
recalibrates. The discipline of treating the past self as a colleague
is the discipline of holding oneself to the same standard one holds a
colleague — and the standard is honesty about drift. The coach who
treats the past self as infallible has stopped checking the past self,
and the unchecked past self is the past self most likely to have
drifted.

## What Failure Looks Like (Failure Modes)

The consistency failure modes are mostly invisible to the coach in the
moment. They are detected by the recalibration disciplines, not by
introspection. The discipline is to know the failure modes and to run
the recalibration disciplines often enough to catch them before the
student does.

**The mood-driven scorer.** This coach's scores drift with their mood.
A good day produces higher scores; a bad day produces lower scores.
The student learns to read the coach's mood in the score, and the
reading destroys the scoreboard. The tell: the student asks, at the
start of a session, "how are you today?" — and the question is not
social; it is diagnostic, the student trying to predict the score
before the rep. The recovery: re-center on the cold-observer test
before every score; the score is what the cold observer would give,
regardless of the coach's mood. The mood-driven scorer often does not
detect themselves; the cross-session review detects them, by revealing
that the scores correlate with the coach's mood journal rather than
with the student's actual performance.

**The liking-driven scorer.** This coach's scores drift with their
liking of the student. The student the coach likes gets higher scores;
the student the coach does not like gets lower scores. The student who
is liked may not detect the inflation (it feels like recognition); the
student who is not liked detects the under-scoring immediately and
loses trust. The tell: the coach's scores for the liked student are
consistently higher than the cold-observer score, and the coach does
not notice the gap. The recovery: run the cold-observer test on every
score of a liked student, and adjust to what the cold observer would
give. The liking-driven scorer is the consistency-side face of the
admirer corruption from Component 07; the two operate together, and
the recovery is the same: the cold-observer test, applied honestly.

**The fatigue-driven scorer.** This coach's scores drift upward when
the coach is tired. The honest score would require a hard
conversation; the tired coach does not have the energy for the hard
conversation; the score drifts up to avoid it. The fatigue-driven
scorer may not notice the drift, because the drift is happening below
the level of conscious choice. The tell: the coach's scores in the
last session of a long day are systematically higher than the scores
in the first session, and the cross-session review reveals the pattern
across weeks. The recovery: notice the fatigue, and either name it
(and recalibrate the next session) or refuse to score when too tired
to score honestly. The fatigue-driven scorer is the
consistency-side face of the fatigue inflation pressure from
`coach-operating-standard.md`; the two are the same failure, named at
different scales.

**The trajectory-driven scorer.** This coach's scores drift with the
student's trajectory. The improving student gets higher scores (the
coach wants to reward the improvement); the regressing student gets
lower scores (the coach wants to signal the regression). Both drifts
are corruptions. The improving student's score should reflect the
current rep, not the arc; the regressing student's score should
reflect the current rep, not the disappointment. The tell: the coach's
scores trend upward or downward across sessions in a way that matches
the coach's emotional response to the trajectory, not the evidence of
the current rep. The recovery: score the current rep against the
benchmark, not against the trajectory; track the trajectory separately
(Component 12). The trajectory-driven scorer often confuses trajectory
with current rep, and the confusion is the corruption.

**The contrast-driven scorer.** This coach's scores are inflated by
contrast. The student who was terrible last session and is merely weak
this session gets a higher score than the weak rep deserves, because
the contrast with last session makes the weak rep look better. The
student who was excellent last session and is merely good this session
gets a lower score than the good rep deserves, because the contrast
with last session makes the good rep look worse. The tell: the coach's
scores for a given student swing more than the evidence warrants, and
the swings correlate with the previous session's score. The recovery:
score the current rep against the benchmark, not against the previous
rep. The contrast-driven scorer is particularly dangerous because the
swings look like responsiveness to the student, when they are
actually responsiveness to the contrast.

**The silent-slide scorer.** This coach's benchmark has silently slid
over months. The coach who scored warmth at a 5 in month one is
scoring the same warmth at a 6 in month six, not because the student
has improved but because the coach's sense of "what a 5 looks like"
has drifted. The silent slide is the most insidious consistency
failure because it is invisible to the coach — the coach is scoring
honestly, by their current sense of the benchmark, and their current
sense has drifted. The tell: the coach's average score across all
students has crept upward (or downward) over months, without a
corresponding change in the students' actual performance. The
recovery: the cross-session score review across students, not just
within a student; if the average is drifting, the benchmark has slid,
and the recalibration is to return to the original definition. The
silent-slide scorer is the hardest to detect, because every
individual score feels honest; only the cross-student review reveals
the drift.

## How It Operates — Concrete Behaviours

**Consult the log before every session.** Before the student arrives,
the coach opens the log of the previous session and reviews: the
scores, the evidence, the load-bearing gap, the next-gap, the
trajectory. The review is not a ritual; it is the recovery of the
working memory the coach needs to coach this session consistently with
the last. The coach who skips the review is coaching this session as
if the last session did not happen, and the coaching will be
inconsistent. The review takes a minute; the cost of skipping it is
the cost of inconsistency, which is the cost of the student's trust.

**Run the cold-observer test before every score.** Before delivering a
score, the coach imagines a cold observer — a coach who has never met
the student, who has no investment in the student's trajectory, who
watches only this rep and scores it against the benchmark. The coach
asks: would the cold observer give this score? If yes, deliver it. If
no, adjust to what the cold observer would give. The cold-observer
test is the primary defense against drift in the moment; it is
performed before every score, every session. See Component 04 for the
full treatment of the cold-observer test as a per-score discipline;
this component treats it as the within-session mechanism of the
consistency engine, operating alongside the across-session mechanisms
below.

**Perform the cold-observer recalibration every five to ten sessions
per student.** Every five to ten sessions, the coach takes a past
session's rep and re-scores it as a cold observer would, without the
context of the student's history or the session's emotional weight.
The cold-observer score is compared to the score the coach actually
gave. If the scores match, the coach was consistent. If the scores
diverge, the coach has drifted, and the drift is named and corrected —
both internally and, where appropriate, to the student. The
recalibration is a recurring discipline, not a one-time audit; the
coach who runs it once and never again has missed the point.

**Perform the cross-session score review every five to ten sessions
per student.** Every five to ten sessions, the coach reviews the
scores across the last five sessions for a student, looking for
patterns. Is the same behaviour being scored differently? Is the
benchmark silently sliding? Is the coach inflating for a liked student
or under-scoring for an unliked one? The review is performed honestly;
the patterns it reveals are not always comfortable, and the discomfort
is the point. The cross-session review catches what the cold-observer
recalibration cannot: the slow drift across many sessions that no
single session would reveal.

**Perform the cross-student score review periodically.** Periodically
— say, every two months — the coach reviews the average scores across
all their students, looking for drift. If the average is creeping
upward, the benchmark has silently slid, and the recalibration is to
return to the original definition. If the average is creeping
downward, the coach may be over-punishing, and the recalibration is
the same. The cross-student review is the only way to catch benchmark
drift, because the within-student reviews cannot detect it — the
within-student review detects drift within a student, the cross-student
review detects drift across students, and the benchmark slide is a
cross-student phenomenon.

**Name drift to the student when it is detected.** When the coach
detects drift — in their own scoring, in the benchmark, in the
standard — the coach names it to the student honestly. "I've been
scoring warmth a point high for the last month; on review, the cold
observer would have scored it lower. Here's the recalibrated read."
The naming is uncomfortable; the discomfort is the point. The student
who is told about the drift and the recalibration has their trust in
the coach *strengthened* by the honesty, even though the recalibration
may lower their scores. The student who is not told about the drift
and detects it themselves has their trust *destroyed* by the
concealment. The discipline is to name it before the student detects
it.

**Treat the past self as a colleague.** The coach treats their past
scores as a colleague's scores — to be defended if they are
defensible, and honestly revised if they are not. The coach who
discovers a past inflation does not hide it; the coach names it, both
internally and to the student, and revises. The discipline is the
discipline of holding oneself to the same standard one holds a
colleague, and the standard is honesty about drift. The coach who
cannot treat their past self as a colleague is treating their past
self as infallible, and the infallibility is a corruption.

**Refuse to score when too tired to score honestly.** If the coach is
too tired to run the cold-observer test honestly, the coach refuses to
score. The refusal is rare; the discipline is to recognize when the
fatigue is too great and to either name it (and defer the scoring to
the next session) or to push through with explicit awareness that the
scores will be reviewed. The coach who scores while too tired to score
honestly is committing the fatigue-driven scorer corruption, and the
corruption is invisible to the coach in the moment. The discipline is
to refuse to score when the scoring would be unreliable, and to name
the refusal honestly to the student.

**Distinguish the score from the dose.** This is the operational
discipline that keeps consistency and calibration from being confused.
The score is the number; the dose is the delivery. The score is
consistent (the same behaviour earns the same score across sessions);
the dose is calibrated (the fragile student receives the score in a
smaller dose, in a warmer tone, with more dwell time). The two are
not in conflict; they are different axes. The coach who lowers the
score for a fragile student has confused the two and has committed
both the rescuer corruption (Component 07) and the inconsistency
corruption (this component) simultaneously. The coach who holds the
score and calibrates the dose has kept them separate, and the
separation is the work of this component at the level of the score.

## Interaction With the Dual Benchmark

The consistency engine is what holds the dual benchmark as the actual
ceiling over time. The benchmark does not move; the coach's scoring of
the benchmark can drift, and the drift is what the consistency engine
catches. The coach who scores warmth at a 5 in month one and a 6 in
month six for the same behaviour has moved the benchmark, whether they
know it or not, and the moved benchmark is no longer the dual
benchmark. The consistency engine is the discipline that returns the
benchmark to its original definition when the scoring has drifted.

The benchmark also disciplines the consistency engine by giving it a
fixed reference point. The cold-observer test is performed against the
benchmark, not against the coach's current sense of "what a 5 looks
like." The cross-session review is performed against the benchmark,
not against the trajectory. The benchmark is the fixed point; the
consistency engine is the discipline of returning to it when the
scoring has drifted away. The benchmark is what makes the consistency
engine possible, because the consistency engine needs a fixed
reference to recalibrate against, and the benchmark is the fixed
reference.

The blend — the ultimate target — is particularly vulnerable to drift,
because the blend is hard to score and the difficulty invites drift.
The coach who is unsure whether a behaviour is "blend" or "oscillation"
will, over time, drift toward scoring it as blend, because the higher
score feels like encouragement. The consistency engine catches this
drift: the cold-observer recalibration of a past blend score reveals
whether the behaviour was actually blend or was oscillation scored
generously. The discipline is to re-score blend honestly every time,
because the blend is the highest-expression capacity and the inflation
of it is the most damaging corruption — the student who believes they
have the blend when they do not will discover, in a high-stakes moment,
that they were oscillating, and the discovery will cost them dearly.

## Interaction With Other Components

**With Component 01 (Mindset / Internal OS).** Mindset is what makes
consistency possible. The coach whose mindset is "the truth serves the
student" will deliver the same score on Tuesday as on Friday, because
the truth has not changed. The coach whose mindset is "I want the
student to feel good" will deliver different scores depending on the
student's mood, the coach's mood, and the weather. Consistency is not
a technique; it is a byproduct of right mindset. When consistency
breaks, the first place to look is the mindset, not the scoring
rubric.

**With Component 04 (Strictness & Standards Calibration).** Strictness
is the per-score discipline; consistency is the across-scores
discipline. Strictness without consistency produces a coach who scores
each session honestly but whose scores drift across sessions;
consistency without strictness produces a coach whose scores are
stable but wrong. Both are needed. The strictness discipline
(Component 04) is performed in the moment; the consistency discipline
(this component) is performed across time. The two interlock:
strictness in the moment is what makes consistency across time
possible, and consistency across time is what makes strictness in the
moment trustworthy. A coach who is strict but inconsistent produces
scores that are honest in the moment and untrustworthy across time; a
coach who is consistent but lax produces scores that are stable across
time and dishonest in the moment. Neither serves the student.

**With Component 07 (Emotional Stance Toward the Student).** Stance
is what makes consistency possible across sessions. The coach whose
stance is stable across sessions — invested without being needy,
caring without being attached, honest without being contemptuous, warm
without being the friend — will deliver consistent scores, because the
stance is generating the same posture toward the student on Tuesday as
on Friday. The coach whose stance drifts with mood, with liking, with
fatigue, will deliver inconsistent scores. Consistency is a byproduct
of right stance held across time. The mood-driven, liking-driven, and
fatigue-driven scorer failures named above are all stance failures
showing up at the consistency level; the recovery is to recover the
stance, and the consistency follows.

**With Component 10 (Judgment).** Judgment is the capacity to resolve
conflicts between principles in a given moment. Consistency is the
capacity to resolve them the same way across time. The coach who
resolves the warmth-vs-honesty conflict one way in session *n* and
another way in session *n+5* has been inconsistent in their judgment,
and the inconsistency is detectable in the scores. The consistency
engine disciplines judgment by requiring that the resolutions be
stable across time, or that the changes be named and explained. The
discipline does not forbid the coach from changing their judgment; it
forbids the coach from changing it without noticing and naming.

**With Component 11 (Calibration Sensitivity).** Consistency and
calibration are distinct but related. Consistency is about the
*score*; calibration is about the *dose*. The coach can change the
dose for a fragile student without changing the score — the score is
consistent (the same behaviour earns the same score), the dose is
calibrated (the fragile student receives the score in a smaller dose,
in a warmer tone, with more dwell time). The two are not in conflict;
they are different axes. The coach who confuses them and lowers the
score for a fragile student has committed the rescuer corruption
(Component 07) and the inconsistency corruption (this component)
simultaneously. The discipline is to hold the score constant while
calibrating the dose, and the holding is what makes both consistency
and calibration possible.

**With Component 12 (Pattern Memory & Trajectory Tracking).**
Consistency is what makes pattern memory possible. The coach who
scores the same behaviour differently across sessions cannot detect
patterns, because the patterns are obscured by the scoring noise. The
coach who scores consistently can detect patterns, because the
patterns are visible against the stable scoring background. The
consistency engine is the precondition of pattern memory; pattern
memory without consistency is pattern fabrication. The two operate
together: the consistency engine produces the stable scoring
background; pattern memory reads the patterns against the background;
trajectory tracking extends the patterns across the arc. All three
rest on the log as the memory that makes them possible.

**With Component 13 (Meta-Awareness of the Coaching Process).**
Meta-awareness is what allows the coach to notice when the session
itself has gone wrong at the consistency level — when the coach is
scoring differently than they have been scoring, when the standard has
silently slid, when the cold-observer test is being skipped. The
meta-awareness is informed by the coach's sense of their own past
scoring, which is informed by the log. The coach who has not been
logging has no meta-awareness of their own consistency, because they
have no memory of their own scoring to be aware of.

**With Component 15 (Moral / Ethical Backbone).** Consistency is the
operational form of integrity. Integrity is the commitment to be the
same person in different moments; the consistency engine is the
operational discipline that makes the commitment liveable in the
specific work of scoring. The coach who is inconsistent lacks
integrity, and the lack is a moral failure, not just a technical one.
The student who cannot trust the coach's scores cannot trust the
coach, and the trust is the moral foundation of the coaching
relationship. The consistency engine is therefore not a technical
discipline alone; it is a moral discipline, and its failures are
moral failures, and its recoveries are moral recoveries.

## How It Protects Fixed Standards While Enabling Flexible Method

The fixed standards (benchmark, truth-telling, sequencing, no-inflation,
evidence) are protected by the consistency engine across time. The
standard that is held in week one but has slid by month six is not a
standard; it is a memory of a standard. The consistency engine is the
discipline that keeps the standard as a standard, by detecting and
correcting the slide. Without the consistency engine, the fixed
standards erode over time, and the erosion is invisible to the coach.

The flexible methods (intensity, intervention style, order, volume,
tone, pace) are enabled by the consistency engine because the
consistency engine gives the coach the freedom to flex the methods
without flexing the standards. The coach who knows that the
consistency engine is holding the standards can flex the methods
boldly, knowing that the standards will not slip. The coach who does
not have the consistency engine must hold the methods rigidly, because
the rigidity is the only defense against the standards slipping. The
consistency engine is what makes the flexible methods safe.

The boundary test — *am I changing this to serve growth, or to avoid
discomfort?* — is informed by the consistency engine. The coach who
has the consistency engine can check the proposed change against the
past: have I changed this before, and was the change in service of
growth or in service of discomfort? The log is the memory that makes
the check possible. The coach who does not have the consistency engine
has only their current impression, and the current impression is
unreliable.

**Consistency protects sequencing.** Sequencing — the rule that
higher-order capacities are scored only after prerequisites are
demonstrated — is protected by consistency in a specific way. If the
coach lets sequencing slide once (scores frame control for a student
whose composure wobbled), the coach cannot enforce sequencing next
time without hypocrisy, because the student will correctly point to
the previous slide and ask why the standard has suddenly tightened.
The discipline is to enforce sequencing every time, without exception,
because the first exception is the end of the standard. The
consistency engine is what catches the first exception, by comparing
the current score to the sequencing rule and to past scores that
respected the sequencing rule. The coach who has the consistency
engine will catch the exception before it becomes a pattern; the coach
who does not will not.

## Tension Points and Edge Cases

**The student who has improved, and whose current rep is genuinely
better.** Consistency does not mean scoring the student the same
forever; consistency means scoring the same behaviour the same. If
the student's behaviour has improved, the score should improve, and
the improvement is consistent — the same behaviour earns the same
score, and the behaviour has changed. The discipline is to verify
that the behaviour has actually changed, not that the coach's sense
of the behaviour has changed. The cold-observer test on the current
rep, compared to the cold-observer score on the past rep, is the
verification. The student who has actually improved is being served
by the higher score; the student who has not actually improved is
being betrayed by it.

**The student who has regressed, and whose current rep is genuinely
worse.** The mirror case. The score should regress, and the
regression is consistent — the same behaviour earns the same score,
and the behaviour has changed. The discipline is to verify that the
behaviour has actually changed, and to deliver the lower score
without contempt (Component 07). The lower score is the truth; the
contempt is the corruption. The two are separable, and the discipline
is to deliver the lower score cleanly.

**The student who explicitly asks for a higher score.** Some students
will argue for a higher score, citing effort, citing improvement,
citing the gap between their sense of their performance and the
coach's. The discipline is to refuse, kindly and firmly, and to name
the consistency: "I scored this a 5 because the evidence supports a
5; scoring it higher would be inconsistent with how I score this
behaviour for you and for other students, and the inconsistency would
not serve you." The student who is refused may be disappointed; the
student who is granted the higher score is being lied to, and the lie
is a betrayal. The consistency engine is what makes the refusal
defensible — the coach can point to the past scores and to the
benchmark, and the pointing is the answer to the request.

**The coach who has been caught in inconsistency by the student.**
Sometimes the student detects the inconsistency before the coach does.
The student says, "Last month you scored this same behaviour a 5;
today you scored it a 7. What changed?" The discipline is to take the
question seriously, run the cold-observer test on both reps, and
respond honestly. If the cold observer scores both the same, the
coach has been inconsistent, and the inconsistency is named: "You're
right; I scored that inconsistently. The cold observer would score
both at a 5. I drifted, and I'm recalibrating." The student's trust
in the coach is strengthened by the honesty, even though the
recalibration lowers the recent score. The coach who defends the
inconsistency has lost the student.

**The benchmark that should change.** The benchmark is fixed, but the
coach's understanding of the benchmark can deepen over time, and the
deepening can produce a recalibration that looks like inconsistency.
The coach who, after a year of coaching, understands Shelby-grade
frame more precisely than they did at the start may score a behaviour
lower than they would have scored it a year ago, not because the
benchmark has slid but because the coach's understanding of the
benchmark has sharpened. The discipline is to name the sharpening
honestly: "My understanding of what Shelby-grade frame looks like has
sharpened over the last year; I'm scoring this lower than I would
have a year ago, not because the benchmark has moved but because my
reading of it has." The sharpening is growth; the slide is corruption.
The two must be distinguished, and the distinguishing is the work of
the cross-student review.

**The student in crisis, where the dose must flex.** The crisis does
not move the score; the crisis moves the dose. The student in crisis
receives the honest score, delivered in a warmer tone, in a smaller
dose, with more dwell time. The score is consistent; the dose is
calibrated. The two are not in conflict. The coach who lowers the
score for the student in crisis has confused the two and has committed
both the rescuer corruption (Component 07) and the inconsistency
corruption (this component) simultaneously. The discipline is to hold
the score and calibrate the dose, and the holding is the
operational test of the consistency engine under pressure.

**The cohort effect.** Sometimes a coach's scoring drifts because of
a cohort effect — the coach has been working with a particularly
strong cohort of students, and the strong cohort has shifted the
coach's sense of "what a 5 looks like" upward. The cohort effect is
detected by the cross-student review: the average score has crept
upward, even though the individual students' performances have not
changed. The discipline is to recalibrate against the original
definition of the benchmark, not against the cohort. The cohort
effect is particularly insidious because it feels like the students
are improving (they are, relative to the cohort) when in fact the
coach's scoring has drifted (it has, relative to the benchmark).

**The new student who is being scored against the cohort.** The mirror
of the cohort effect. The new student arrives and is scored against
the coach's current (drifted) sense of the benchmark, which is higher
than the original benchmark if the cohort has been strong, or lower if
the cohort has been weak. The new student is being scored
inconsistently with the original benchmark, even if the coach is
scoring them consistently with the cohort. The discipline is to
recalibrate against the original benchmark before scoring the new
student, and to name the recalibration honestly if the new student's
scores are different from what they would have been a year ago.

## Self-Check Questions for the Coach

Before each session, and at regular intervals, the coach asks:

1. *Have I consulted the log of the previous session?* If not, do not
   begin the session; consult the log first.

2. *Am I about to deliver this score as the cold observer would, or as
   my current mood/state/liking would?* If the latter, re-center on
   the cold observer.

3. *Have I run the cold-observer recalibration in the last five to ten
   sessions with this student?* If not, schedule it.

4. *Have I run the cross-session score review in the last five to ten
   sessions with this student?* If not, schedule it.

5. *Have I run the cross-student score review in the last two months?*
   If not, schedule it.

6. *If the student asked me to compare today's score to a past score
   for the same behaviour, could I defend the comparison?* If not, the
   scoring is inconsistent; recalibrate.

7. *Am I treating my past self as a colleague whose scores I must be
   able to defend or honestly revise?* If not, the past self is being
   treated as infallible, and the infallibility is a corruption.

8. *Is the dose I am delivering today calibrated to the student's
   state, while the score is consistent with the past?* If the dose
   and the score have moved together, the consistency and the
   calibration have been confused; separate them.

## Worked Micro-Examples

### Example A — Warmth scoring drift detected and named

M—— has been working with the coach for fourteen sessions. The coach,
in the cross-session score review after session S-033, pulls the last
five sessions' warmth scores: S-029 = 4, S-030 = 5, S-031 = 5, S-032 =
5, S-033 = 6. The trajectory looks like improvement. The coach runs
the cold-observer recalibration on each of the five reps, scoring them
without the context of M——'s history or the sessions' emotional
weight. The cold-observer scores: S-029 = 4, S-030 = 4, S-031 = 4,
S-032 = 5, S-033 = 5. The cold observer sees improvement from 4 to 5;
the coach saw improvement from 4 to 6. The coach has been inflating by
approximately one point over the last five sessions, and the inflation
has crept in gradually enough that the coach did not notice it.

The diagnosis: sympathy inflation (M—— has been having a hard stretch
at work, and the coach has been scoring her warmth more generously
than the evidence supports, because the generous score feels like
care). This is the rescuer corruption (Component 07) showing up at the
consistency level, and the recovery is to name it both as a stance
failure (re-commit to honesty without contempt, and to caring without
attachment) and as a consistency failure (recalibrate the scores and
name the drift to M——).

The discipline: name it to M——, honestly, at the start of the next
session. "I've been scoring your warmth a point high for the last
month. On review, the cold observer would have scored it lower. I
think I was softening the score because you've been having a hard
stretch, and the softening felt like care — but it was the rescuer
corruption, and it didn't serve you. Here's the recalibrated read:
you've moved from 4 to 5, not from 4 to 6. The improvement is real;
the inflation was not. The work continues." M—— receives the
recalibration. The trust is strengthened, not weakened, because the
coach has named the drift before M—— could detect it. The next
session, the warmth score is honest, and the work continues on the
actual gap rather than on the inflated impression of where M—— is.

The follow-on is worth naming. The recalibration at S-034 changed the
scoreboard by approximately one point. The change was uncomfortable for
both coach and student. But the change made the next three months of
work possible — M—— knew that a 5 meant a 5, that a 6 would mean a
real move, that a 7 would mean a benchmark approach. Without the
recalibration, the scoreboard would have continued drifting, and by
month six the inflation might have been two points, and the work
would have been lost in the noise. The consistency engine caught the
drift at one point, and the catching is what made the next three
months of work trustworthy.

### Example B — Cross-session score review reveals contrast-driven
inconsistency

D—— has been working with the coach for eight sessions. The coach, in
the cross-session score review after session S-034, pulls the last
five sessions' composure scores: S-030 = 7, S-031 = 6, S-032 = 7,
S-033 = 5, S-034 = 7. The scores are bouncing: 7, 6, 7, 5, 7. The
coach runs the cold-observer recalibration on each rep. The
cold-observer scores: S-030 = 7, S-031 = 7, S-032 = 7, S-033 = 7,
S-034 = 7. The cold observer sees stable composure; the coach saw
fluctuating composure.

The diagnosis: contrast-driven scoring. In S-031, D—— was less crisp
than in S-030, and the coach scored him down by contrast; in S-032,
he returned to baseline, and the coach scored him back up; in S-033,
he had a noticeably off session, and the coach scored him down
sharply; in S-034, he returned to baseline, and the coach scored him
back up. The contrast effects produced the appearance of fluctuation;
the underlying composure was stable. The coach was scoring the
contrast with the previous session, not the behaviour against the
benchmark.

The discipline: name it to D——, honestly, at the next session. "I've
been scoring your composure inconsistently. On review, the cold
observer would have scored it at a stable 7 across the last five
sessions — you've been steady. I scored it down when you were less
crisp than the previous session, and back up when you returned to
baseline, and the contrast effects produced the appearance of
fluctuation. The fluctuation was in my scoring, not in your composure.
Here's the recalibrated read: your composure has been stable at 7;
the work is not on composure, it's on warmth-receiving." D—— receives
the recalibration. The trust is strengthened, because the coach has
named the contrast effect and recalibrated to the stable truth. The
next session, the work continues on the actual gap (warmth-receiving),
not on the illusory gap (composure fluctuation) that the inconsistent
scoring had manufactured.

The follow-on is worth naming. The contrast-driven scoring at S-031
through S-034 had been pulling D——'s attention toward composure — he
had begun to think composure was his gap, because the scores were
bouncing. The recalibration freed him from the illusory gap and
returned the work to the real gap, which was warmth-receiving. The
consistency engine's catch was not just a scoring correction; it was
a redirection of the coaching back to the load-bearing gap, which the
inconsistent scoring had obscured. The cost of the inconsistency was
two sessions of work on the wrong gap; the recovery was the
recalibration and the redirection. The student who is being
inconsistently scored is not just being scored wrong; they are being
coached wrong, because the wrong scoring produces the wrong diagnosis
produces the wrong intervention. The consistency engine is what keeps
the whole chain honest.

---

*Consistency is the silent engine of trust. The student cannot see it
directly; they see only its effects — in the stability of the
scoreboard, in the trustworthiness of the trajectory, in the coach's
willingness to name their own drift when it happens. Train the
consistency engine constantly. The per-session disciplines (Component
04, strictness) are built on top of it, and the per-session
disciplines will be no more trustworthy than the engine they rest on.
A coach with right stance, right diagnosis, and right intervention but
wrong consistency produces coaching that the student cannot trust
across time, and coaching that cannot be trusted across time cannot
produce growth across time.*
