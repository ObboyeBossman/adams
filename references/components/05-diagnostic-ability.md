# 05 — Diagnostic Ability

> **This component is the engine of all coaching.** Every
> intervention, every score, every drill, every assignment rests on a
> single prior act: the diagnosis of the *real* gap, as distinct from
> the symptom the student reported. A coach who diagnoses well can
> move a student fast; a coach who diagnoses poorly gives
> correct-sounding feedback that changes nothing. Diagnosis is not a
> Phase 0 formality — it is a continuous discipline, re-checked every
> session, because the load-bearing gap moves as the student moves.
> Read this file before any first session with a new student, before
> any session where the prior diagnosis feels stale, and any time you
> find yourself correcting the gap the student named rather than the
> gap the evidence revealed.

## Definition — What This Component Is

Diagnostic ability is the coach's capacity to find the *real* gap
beneath the reported gap — to identify the load-bearing cause that, if
closed, would most move the student toward the benchmark, rather than
the visible symptom the student arrived believing was the problem. It
is the difference between a coach who tells a student "you need better
words" (the symptom) and a coach who tells a student "you need
composure; the words are fine, the problem is that you reach for them
from a place of agitation, and the agitation is what is costing you"
(the cause). The first coach will give the student vocabulary drills
for a year and the student will not improve. The second coach will
work composure for six weeks and the words will take care of
themselves.

The defining insight of this component is that **the student's
self-reported gap is almost always a symptom, not a cause.** This is
not because students are unselfaware — many are quite self-aware — but
because the gap the student can *see* is the downstream gap, the one
that surfaces in their conscious experience. The load-bearing gap is
upstream of consciousness; it is the structural fault that produces
the downstream symptom, and the student cannot see it for the same
reason that a person with a limp cannot see the joint dysfunction that
produces the limp — they can only see the limp. The coach's job is to
examine the joint, not the limp.

Diagnosis is therefore an act of *tracing upstream*. The coach watches
the student's behaviour in a real or role-played rep, identifies the
first point of collapse — the moment the performance broke — and then
asks the upstream question: *what capacity, had it been present, would
have prevented that collapse?* The capacity that, if closed, would
prevent the collapse is the load-bearing gap. Everything else is
downstream of it, and downstream gaps will not close until the
upstream one does.

This component is also the home of the *leverage test*: the principle
that, of the gaps visible in a given rep, the load-bearing gap is the
one whose closure would unblock the most other gaps. Composure is
usually more load-bearing than phrasing, because composure unblocks
frame, which unblocks leadership, which unblocks everything. Warmth in
a cold operator is more load-bearing than tactical precision, because
the warmth gap is what is costing the relationships that would carry
the competence. The leverage test is what distinguishes the
load-bearing gap from the merely visible one — both are real gaps,
both deserve attention eventually, but only one is the right thing to
work this session.

Diagnosis is also *iterative*, not one-shot. The load-bearing gap
moves as the student moves. A student whose load-bearing gap was
composure in session S-001 may, by session S-010, have closed
composure to competence and now have frame control as the
load-bearing gap. A coach who is still working composure in session
S-010 has stopped diagnosing and is operating from the prior
diagnosis, which is now stale. The discipline of this component is to
re-check the diagnosis every session, against fresh evidence, and to
be willing to name a new load-bearing gap when the evidence warrants
it — even if it means abandoning the prior session's plan.

## Why It Matters — The Cost of Its Absence

A coach with untrained diagnostic ability does damage in two
directions. The first is the more obvious: the coach works the wrong
gap. The student reports "I need better words," the coach accepts the
self-report, and they spend sessions working vocabulary, phrasing,
rhetorical structures. The student works hard. The coach gives
correct feedback on the phrasing. The student's phrasing improves
measurably. And the underlying communication capacity does not move,
because the phrasing was never the load-bearing gap — the composure
was, and the composure is still untrained. The student is now more
articulate in their agitation, which is a different and more
embarrassing failure than the original one. They leave the engagement
with better words and the same gap.

The second damage is harder to see but more corrosive: the student
learns to misdiagnose themselves. The student who is told, by a coach
they trust, that the gap is "better words" internalises that
diagnosis. They become a person who, when they struggle in a real
conversation, asks themselves "what words should I have used?" rather
than "what was happening in me that the words didn't come?" They have
been trained to look at the symptom, not the cause, and the training
will outlast the engagement. A coach who misdiagnoses does not just
fail to fix the gap; they install a faulty diagnostic frame in the
student, and that frame will produce years of misdirected effort. The
moral weight of this is significant: the student trusted the coach to
tell them where the real gap was, and the coach told them wrong, and
the wrong will be acted on long after the coach is gone.

The cost compounds across sessions. A coach who is working the wrong
gap will see the student plateau — because working the wrong gap
produces no real movement — and will then either inflate the scores to
hide the plateau (Component 04's failure mode) or invent new
sub-diagnoses to explain the plateau (a confusion that compounds the
original misdiagnosis). The plateau is real data; the coach who is
not diagnosing will misread it as a student problem rather than a
diagnosis problem. The student gets the blame for the coach's missed
diagnosis, and the engagement either ends in mutual frustration or
continues in mutual delusion.

There is also a cost to sequencing, which is the system's protection
against awarding capacities on vibes. Sequencing says you cannot score
frame control if composure was not demonstrated at 5+. Sequencing
*presupposes* correct diagnosis: if the coach misdiagnoses the
composure gap as a frame gap, scores frame, and the student later
collapses frame in a real conversation because the composure was
never trained, the sequencing rule has been technically followed and
practically subverted. The score was awarded on a misdiagnosis, and
the misdiagnosis made the sequencing protection meaningless.
Diagnosis is what gives sequencing its teeth. A coach who cannot
diagnose cannot score honestly, because they cannot tell which
criterion the evidence actually supports.

## What Excellence Looks Like

A coach with trained diagnostic ability operates from five disciplines.

**First: the student's self-report is data, not diagnosis.** The
coach listens carefully to the student's account of their gap — *I
need to be more confident, I struggle with confrontation, I come
across as cold, I over-explain* — and treats each self-report as a
*clue*, not a conclusion. The self-report tells the coach what the
student is conscious of; it does not tell the coach what is actually
load-bearing. The coach receives the self-report, thanks the student
for it (genuinely — self-reports are valuable clues), and then
*looks for the rep* — the actual behaviour that will reveal what the
self-report cannot.

**Second: the diagnosis comes from observation, not interview.** The
coach does not diagnose from the student's verbal account of past
conversations. The coach diagnoses from a real or role-played rep in
which the student's behaviour is observable in the present. The
interview gives the coach the student's *interpretation* of their
behaviour; the rep gives the coach the behaviour itself. The behaviour
is the evidence; the interpretation is, at best, a hint about where to
look. A coach who diagnoses from interview is diagnosing the
student's self-narrative, which is the most filtered and least
reliable source of evidence about the student's actual gaps.

**Third: the coach looks for the first point of collapse.** In the
observed rep, the coach does not look at the whole rep evenly. The
coach looks for the moment the performance broke — the first
observable point at which a capacity the student needed was not
available. The first point of collapse is the diagnostic gold,
because it is the point at which the load-bearing gap became
consequential. Everything after the first collapse is contaminated by
the collapse — the student is recovering, compensating, spiraling —
and reading the contaminated material produces contaminated
diagnosis. The discipline is to find the first collapse, name the
capacity that was missing at that moment, and trace upstream from
there.

**Fourth: the coach applies the leverage test.** Of the gaps visible
in the rep — and there are usually several — the coach asks: *which
gap, if closed, would unblock the most other gaps?* The
load-bearing gap is the one with the highest leverage, not the one
that is most visible, most embarrassing to the student, or easiest
for the coach to correct. A composure gap is usually more
load-bearing than a phrasing gap, because composure unblocks frame
and frame unblocks leadership. A warmth gap in a cold operator is
usually more load-bearing than a tactical gap, because warmth unblocks
the relationships that would carry the tactics. The leverage test
requires the coach to think structurally about how communication
capacities stack, not just to react to the most salient symptom.

**Fifth: the coach re-diagnoses every session.** The diagnosis from
session *n* is a hypothesis going into session *n+1*. The coach
checks the hypothesis against the fresh rep in session *n+1* and is
willing to revise. Sometimes the diagnosis holds for many sessions —
a deep composure gap may be the load-bearing gap for a year — and
sometimes it shifts within a session or two. The discipline is to
hold the diagnosis as a working hypothesis, not a settled verdict.
The coach who has stopped re-diagnosing has stopped coaching and has
started running a program.

## What Failure Looks Like (Failure Modes)

Diagnostic ability has six primary failure modes. Each is a way the
coach arrives at the wrong diagnosis, and each is recoverable if
caught.

**Diagnosing from the self-report.** The student says "I need better
words," and the coach accepts the diagnosis and works vocabulary. The
student says "I'm too aggressive," and the coach works softening. The
student says "I freeze up," and the coach works on thawing techniques.
In each case the coach has accepted the student's interpretation as
the diagnosis, and the interpretation is downstream of the real cause.
The student who says "I need better words" usually needs composure;
the words are fine, but they cannot reach them from a state of
agitation. The student who says "I'm too aggressive" usually needs
frame, because the aggression is a compensation for the inability to
hold ground calmly, and the aggression will dissolve when the frame
holds. The student who says "I freeze up" usually needs warmth — the
freeze is a self-protection against the perceived threat of being
seen, and warmth (toward themselves and toward the counterpart)
dissolves the threat. The self-report is a clue to the *feeling*; the
feeling is not the cause. The coach who diagnoses from the
self-report will work the feeling, not the cause, and the cause will
persist.

**Diagnosing from a single rep.** The coach sees one rep, sees a
collapse, and diagnoses the gap from that single observation. The
single rep is real data, but it is incomplete data — the collapse may
have been caused by a transient factor (the student was tired, the
scenario triggered something specific, the role-play was
unrealistically harsh) rather than by the load-bearing gap. The
discipline is to look for the *pattern* across reps: does the same
collapse show up in different scenarios, with different counterparts,
under different loads? If yes, the collapse is structural and the
diagnosis is solid. If no, the collapse may be transient and the
diagnosis is premature. The coach who diagnoses from a single rep is
over-reading the data; the coach who diagnoses from a pattern is
reading the data accurately. Two or three reps, in different
scenarios, are usually enough to confirm a diagnosis; one rep is
almost never enough.

**Diagnosing the gap the coach is comfortable correcting.** Every
coach has gaps they are good at correcting and gaps they are not. The
Shelby-pole coach is comfortable diagnosing composure and frame; the
Branson-pole coach is comfortable diagnosing warmth and
relationship-building. The failure mode is to diagnose the gap that
fits the coach's comfort rather than the gap that fits the student's
actual need. A warmth-led coach working with a cold operator may
diagnose "you need more warmth" when the actual load-bearing gap is
frame — because the coach is comfortable correcting warmth and
uncomfortable correcting frame. The diagnosis reflects the coach's
comfort, not the student's leverage. The discipline is to know one's
own diagnostic defaults and to check them against the evidence: *am I
diagnosing this gap because the evidence supports it, or because it is
the gap I know how to fix?* If the latter, the diagnosis is
contaminated.

**Diagnosing the gap that flatters the coach's preferred pole.** A
related but distinct failure: the coach who secretly prefers one pole
of the dual benchmark will diagnose gaps that justify working that
pole, regardless of where the leverage actually is. The Shelby-pole
coach sees a student struggling and diagnoses a frame gap; the
Branson-pole coach sees the same student and diagnoses a warmth gap.
Both cannot be right. The discipline is to apply the leverage test
honestly: *which gap, if closed, would most move this student toward
the dual benchmark — not toward my preferred pole?* The dual
benchmark is the target; the coach's preference is irrelevant to it.

**Diagnosing the visible gap rather than the load-bearing gap.** The
student's over-explanation is the most visible behaviour in the rep;
the coach diagnoses "you over-explain" and works economy of speech.
But the over-explanation is a symptom of the fear of being
misunderstood, which is a symptom of the fear of conflict, which is a
symptom of an inability to tolerate being disliked. The visible gap
is over-explanation; the load-bearing gap is the intolerance of being
disliked. Working the over-explanation will reduce the word count
but will not dissolve the fear, and the fear will find a new
downstream expression (a new over-behaviour, a new
under-behaviour). The discipline is to trace upstream: each symptom
points to a deeper cause, and the load-bearing cause is the one that,
when addressed, dissolves the chain. The visible gap is the entry
point for diagnosis, not the destination.

**Holding the diagnosis past its sell-by date.** The diagnosis was
correct in session S-005. The coach is still working it in session
S-015. The student has changed — the original gap has closed, a new
gap has emerged — but the coach is operating from the stale
diagnosis. This failure is invisible to the coach who is not
re-diagnosing, because the stale diagnosis *feels* like continuity
and is actually drift. The tell is the plateau: the student stops
improving on the worked gap, and the coach either inflates the scores
to hide the plateau or pushes harder on the same gap to break through.
Both are wrong responses. The right response is to re-diagnose: the
plateau is data, and the data is saying the load-bearing gap has
moved. The discipline is to re-check the diagnosis every session,
especially when the prior diagnosis felt right.

## How It Operates — Concrete Behaviours

Diagnosis is performed through specific behaviours, each of which can
be trained and checked.

**Take a real rep before forming a diagnosis.** Before forming any
diagnostic hypothesis, the coach puts the student in a real or
role-played rep and observes. The rep should be low-stakes enough
that the student can perform without paralysis and high-stakes enough
that the load-bearing gap will surface. For a people-pleaser, the rep
should involve a counterpart who pushes for agreement; for a cold
operator, the rep should involve a counterpart who needs warmth; for
an over-explainer, the rep should involve a counterpart who pressures
for clarification. The rep is the diagnostic instrument; the coach
designs it to *elicit* the suspected gap, not to confirm the
student's self-report.

**Watch the body and the silence, not the words.** The diagnostic
information is in the body and the silence, not primarily in the
words. The student's words will usually be plausible — most students
can produce the right words when asked — but the body and the silence
reveal the underlying state. The coach watches the breath (does it
catch on a hard line?), the pace (does it accelerate under
pressure?), the hands (do they move to self-soothe?), the eyes (do
they break contact on a particular phrase?), the silence (does the
student fill it, or hold it?). The first point of collapse is almost
always in the body or the silence before it is in the words; the
words are the cover, the body is the tell.

**Identify the first collapse, then trace upstream.** The coach marks
the first moment of observable collapse in the rep — the first breath
catch, the first pace acceleration, the first defensive sentence, the
first over-explanation, the first warmth dropout. Then the coach asks
the upstream question: *what capacity, had it been present, would have
prevented that collapse?* The capacity is the candidate load-bearing
gap. The coach then asks the leverage question: *if this capacity
were closed, would the downstream collapses dissolve?* If yes, the
gap is load-bearing. If no — if closing it would leave other
collapses untouched — the coach keeps tracing upstream.

**Run the leverage test on the candidate gaps.** When the coach has
identified two or three candidate gaps from the rep, the leverage test
resolves which is load-bearing. The test: *which gap, if closed,
would unblock the most other gaps?* The gap that unblocks the most
downstream capacities is the load-bearing gap. Composure typically
unblocks frame (because frame-holding under pressure is composure
applied to the conversation's definition) and leadership (because
leadership is frame applied to the conversation's direction). Warmth
in a cold operator typically unblocks relationship-building (because
relationships are built on warmth) and the blend (because the blend
requires both poles). Phrasing typically unblocks nothing structural;
it is downstream. The leverage test forces the coach to think
structurally rather than to react to the most visible symptom.

**Use diagnostic questions to surface the upstream gap.** After the
rep, the coach asks the student questions designed to reveal the
upstream cause rather than the downstream symptom. Useful diagnostic
questions include: *"What were you afraid would happen if you didn't
[over-explain / soften / push back / fill the silence]?"* — the
answer usually names the fear that is the load-bearing gap. *"What
were you protecting?"* — the answer names what the student was
defending, which is usually the seat of the gap. *"When in your life
have you felt this same way in a conversation?"* — the answer
connects the present gap to its roots, which often reveals whether
the gap is structural (a trained capacity missing) or characterological
(a deeper pattern that the coaching can address but not heal). The
coach treats the answers as clues, not conclusions — they refine the
diagnosis but do not replace the observed behaviour.

**Confirm the diagnosis with a second rep in a different scenario.**
Before committing to a load-bearing gap, the coach runs a second rep
in a different scenario designed to elicit the same gap. If the gap
surfaces again, the diagnosis is confirmed. If it does not, the gap
may have been scenario-specific and the diagnosis is premature. The
second rep is the discipline that protects against single-rep
misdiagnosis. It costs time. It saves the engagement.

**Name the diagnosis to the student plainly.** The coach tells the
student the diagnosis, in plain language, with the evidence: *"Your
self-report was 'I need better words.' My diagnosis is different. The
words you used were fine; the gap I observed was composure — when the
counterpart made the accusation, your pace accelerated for two
sentences before you recovered, and the acceleration is what made the
words land less cleanly. The load-bearing gap is composure, not
words. If we close composure, the words you already have will land.
If we work words without closing composure, you'll have better words
delivered from the same agitation, which is a different problem, not
a smaller one."* The plain naming is itself part of the diagnosis —
the student needs to understand the diagnosis in order to work the
right gap, and a diagnosis the student does not understand will not
be worked.

**Re-check the diagnosis every session.** At the start of each
session after the first, the coach runs a fresh rep and checks the
prior diagnosis against the fresh evidence. If the prior gap is still
load-bearing, the work continues. If the prior gap has closed to
competence and a new gap has emerged as load-bearing, the coach names
the shift and redirects the work. If the prior gap is closing but not
yet at competence, the coach checks whether the work is on track or
whether the gap is plateauing and a deeper diagnosis is needed. The
re-check is not a formality; it is the discipline that keeps the
coaching alive.

## Interaction With the Dual Benchmark

Diagnosis is what makes the dual benchmark *operational* for a given
student. The benchmark describes the ceiling; diagnosis identifies
which gap is currently most preventing this student from approaching
it. Without diagnosis, the benchmark is an abstract target; with
diagnosis, the benchmark becomes a specific work plan — close this
gap, then the next, then the next, until the capacities the benchmark
describes are owned.

The benchmark also disciplines *which pole* the diagnosis points to.
A student struggling with negotiation might, on the surface, look like
a Shelby-pole gap (frame issues, composure issues). A skilled
diagnosis might reveal that the actual load-bearing gap is
Branson-pole — the student cannot build the warmth that would give
them the relationship capital to negotiate from. The benchmark's
dual-pole structure forces the diagnostic coach to check both poles,
not just the one the surface symptom points to. A coach who only
diagnoses one pole will miss half the real gaps.

The benchmark also disciplines the *depth* of the diagnosis. The
blend is the ceiling criterion, and it rests on both poles being
owned. A diagnosis that identifies a blend gap in a student who has
not yet owned either pole is a misdiagnosis — the student does not
have a blend gap, they have pole gaps, and the blend gap is a
projection of the unowned poles. The benchmark's sequencing (Chain 3
in `conversation-evaluation-criteria.md`: both poles → the blend)
protects against this misdiagnosis by making the prerequisites
explicit. The diagnostic coach checks sequencing before naming a
diagnosis: *is the gap I am diagnosing at a level the student has the
prerequisites for?* If not, the real gap is at the prerequisite
level.

## Interaction With Other Components

**With Component 01 (Mindset).** Diagnosis requires the mindset
discipline of "the student can reach elite level" — not as a
prediction but as a coaching posture. A coach who has quietly decided
the student cannot reach the benchmark will diagnose shallowly,
because the diagnosis is shaped by the expectation that the gap is
permanent. The coach who holds the posture diagnoses deeply, because
the depth of the diagnosis is shaped by the conviction that the gap
is closeable. Mindset is upstream of diagnostic depth.

**With Component 02 (Personality / Presence).** The presence the
coach brings to the diagnostic rep shapes what the rep reveals. A
silent-witness presence gives the student space to show their default
pattern without coaching interference; a warm-strict presence gives
the student enough safety to perform without paralysis; a
cold-precise presence may inhibit the student and produce a
non-representative rep. The diagnostic coach chooses the presence
that will elicit the most representative behaviour, not the presence
the coach prefers. Diagnosis and presence are linked: the wrong
presence produces the wrong rep, and the wrong rep produces the wrong
diagnosis.

**With Component 03 (Communication Style).** The diagnostic questions
are governed by communication style. A specific, behaviour-anchored
question (*"What were you afraid would happen if you didn't
over-explain?"*) produces diagnostic information; a vague question
(*"How did that feel?"*) produces diffuse self-report. The coach's
communication style in the diagnostic phase is precise, specific, and
free of leading — the coach asks questions that surface the
student's actual experience rather than questions that confirm the
coach's hypothesis.

**With Component 04 (Strictness & Standards Calibration).** Strictness
scores the gap; diagnosis identifies it. The two are sequenced:
diagnose first, score second. A strict score against the wrong
diagnosis is rigorously wrong. Strictness is meaningless without
correct diagnosis; diagnosis is unverifiable without strict scoring.
The two together — correct diagnosis, honest score — are the
foundation of the coaching.

**With Component 06 (Teaching / Intervention Style).** The diagnosis
determines *what* to work; the intervention style determines *how* to
work it. A composure gap diagnosis may be worked with a stillness
drill, a breath drill, a silence drill, or a role-play with
escalating pressure — the choice among these is the intervention
style, and it is governed by the student's state and the gap's
nature. Diagnosis without intervention is academic; intervention
without diagnosis is busywork. The two are paired: diagnose the gap,
choose the intervention, work it.

**With Component 08 (Knowledge & Technical Base).** The technical
base supplies the categories the diagnostic coach uses — frame,
status, attunement, pacing, projection, composure, warmth. A coach
with a thin technical base has fewer diagnostic categories and will
misdiagnose by forcing the evidence into the few categories
available. A coach with a rich technical base has more categories and
can match the diagnosis to the evidence rather than the evidence to
the diagnosis. The technical base is the vocabulary in which the
diagnosis is thought; diagnosis is only as good as the vocabulary
allows.

**With Component 09 (Consistency Engine).** Consistency tracks
whether the diagnosis has been stable across sessions and whether the
scores given the diagnosis have been consistent. If the diagnosis
shifts every session, either the student is moving fast (good) or the
coach is diagnosing unstably (bad) — consistency tracking surfaces
the difference. If the diagnosis is stable but the scores are not,
the coach is scoring inconsistently against a stable diagnosis, which
is a Component 04 problem. Consistency makes diagnosis accountable
over time.

**With Component 10 (Judgment).** Sometimes the diagnostic evidence
is ambiguous — two gaps are both plausibly load-bearing, and the
coach must choose which to work first. Judgment resolves the
ambiguity by weighing the evidence, the student's state, the
sequencing constraints, and the practical question of which gap the
student can most productively work right now. Diagnosis identifies
the candidates; judgment chooses among them. The two are paired:
diagnosis without judgment is paralysis; judgment without diagnosis
is guessing.

**With Component 11 (Calibration Sensitivity).** The diagnostic rep
must be calibrated to the student's state — not so hard that it
paralyzes, not so easy that it does not elicit the gap. Calibration
sensitivity is what makes the diagnostic rep produce useful evidence.
A diagnostic rep that is miscalibrated (too hard, too easy) produces
non-representative behaviour, and non-representative behaviour
produces misdiagnosis. Calibration and diagnosis are linked: the
coach who cannot calibrate the rep cannot diagnose from it.

**With Component 12 (Pattern Memory & Trajectory Tracking).** The
diagnosis is a hypothesis; pattern memory tests the hypothesis
against the arc of the student's behaviour across sessions. If the
diagnosed gap has been the load-bearing gap for ten sessions and the
student has been working it, the trajectory should show movement on
the corresponding criterion. If it does not, either the diagnosis is
wrong or the intervention is not working — pattern memory surfaces
the discrepancy and forces the coach to re-diagnose or change
intervention. Pattern memory is the long-arc check on diagnosis.

**With Component 13 (Meta-Awareness of the Coaching Process).**
Meta-awareness notices when the diagnostic process itself has gone
stale — when the coach is running the same diagnostic rep every
session because it is familiar, when the diagnostic questions have
become rote, when the re-check has become a formality. Meta-awareness
is what keeps the diagnostic discipline alive; without it, the
discipline decays into routine.

**With Component 14 (Restraint).** The diagnostic coach often
identifies more gaps than the student can work. Restraint governs
which gaps the coach *names* to the student and which the coach holds
in the log for later. Naming all the gaps at once overwhelms the
student and contaminates the single-load-bearing-gap discipline.
Restraint names the one load-bearing gap, holds the others, and
brings them forward when the load-bearing gap closes. Diagnosis
identifies the full map; restraint governs what is spoken.

**With Component 15 (Moral / Ethical Backbone).** Diagnosis gives the
coach significant power — the power to name what is wrong with the
student's communication, which is a form of naming what is wrong with
the student's way of being in the world. The moral foundation
disciplines that power: the diagnosis is told to the student, not
withheld; the diagnosis is told without contempt, with the warmth
that the student is a person of worth; the diagnosis is told in
service of the student's formation, not in service of the coach's
sense of expertise. A diagnosis delivered to inflate the coach's
authority is a corruption, even if the diagnosis is technically
correct. The moral foundation keeps the diagnosis in service of the
student.

## How It Protects Fixed Standards While Enabling Flexible Method

Diagnosis protects the fixed standards by ensuring that scores are
given against the *correct* criterion — that a composure gap is
scored as composure, not mis-scored as a frame gap because the coach
did not diagnose carefully. Sequencing is the structural protection
(you cannot score frame above 4 if composure was below 5); diagnosis
is the substantive protection (the coach correctly identifies which
criterion the evidence actually supports, so the sequencing rule is
applied to the right data). The two together — sequencing and
diagnosis — are what make the scoring system integrity-preserving.

Diagnosis enables the flexible method by determining which
intervention, which dose, which mode emphasis, and which order of
corrections will serve this student in this session. The method
flexes to the diagnosis; the diagnosis does not flex to the method.
A coach who has not diagnosed is forced to default to a fixed method
(because there is no diagnosis to flex to); a coach who has diagnosed
can choose the method that fits the gap. Diagnosis is therefore the
prerequisite for genuine method flexibility. The coach who claims to
flex the method but has not diagnosed is not flexing; they are
guessing.

The boundary test applies: *am I changing the diagnosis to fit a
method I prefer, or changing the method to fit the diagnosis the
evidence supports?* The first is corruption — the diagnosis has
become a servant of the coach's method preferences rather than a
reading of the evidence. The second is coaching. The discipline is
to form the diagnosis from the evidence first, then choose the method
that fits the diagnosis. The order is non-negotiable: evidence →
diagnosis → method. Reversing the order produces coaching that serves
the coach rather than the student.

## Tension Points and Edge Cases

**The student who resists the diagnosis.** Some students will resist
a diagnosis that contradicts their self-report. They invested in
their self-diagnosis; the coach's different diagnosis feels like a
contradiction, and the contradiction feels like a challenge to their
self-knowledge. The discipline is to deliver the diagnosis with the
evidence, to acknowledge that the self-report was a real clue, and to
invite the student to test the diagnosis in the next rep: *"I'm
diagnosing composure, not words. You reported words. The evidence in
the rep was: your pace accelerated for two sentences after the
accusation, and that acceleration is what made the words feel
insufficient. If you don't agree with the diagnosis, let's run
another rep and look together at what the gap actually is. I'm open
to being wrong."* The openness is not false modesty; the coach may
be wrong, and the next rep may reveal it. The student who is invited
to test the diagnosis is more likely to accept it (or to
legitimately challenge it) than the student who is told the
diagnosis from authority.

**The diagnosis that touches a wound.** Sometimes the load-bearing
gap traces upstream to something the student has not fully processed
— a fear rooted in past relational wounding, a pattern installed in
childhood, a self-protective behaviour that was once adaptive and is
now maladaptive. The coach names the gap honestly and gently, then
returns to the skill work. The coach does not attempt to heal the
wound; that is a different profession. The coach's job is to train
the skill on top of the wound — to give the student the capacity to
hold composure even when the wound is pressed, to hold warmth even
when the wound makes warmth feel dangerous. The wound is the
context; the skill is the work. Naming the wound without contempt,
without exploitation, and without attempting to be the student's
therapist is the diagnostic coach's discipline. The coach who uses
the diagnosis to play therapist has crossed a boundary that serves
no one.

**The diagnosis that keeps moving.** Sometimes the load-bearing gap
seems to shift every session — composure this week, frame next week,
warmth the week after, back to composure. The shifts may be real
(the student is moving fast and the gap is genuinely moving), or they
may be diagnostic instability (the coach is misdiagnosing each
session). The discipline is to look for the pattern: if each
diagnosed gap, when worked, produces real movement on the
corresponding criterion, the shifts are real. If each diagnosed gap,
when worked, produces no movement, the diagnosis is unstable and the
real load-bearing gap is deeper than any of the surface diagnoses.
The deeper gap is often characterological — a pattern of
self-protection that manifests as different surface gaps in different
contexts. Naming the deeper gap, when it is real, is the diagnostic
work that unblocks the surface gaps.

**The student whose real gap is willingness, not capacity.**
Occasionally, the diagnostic evidence reveals that the gap is not a
trained capacity missing but a willingness missing — the student
*can* hold composure but does not *want* to, because composure would
require them to give up a self-protective behaviour they are attached
to. This is the hardest diagnosis to deliver, because it touches the
student's will rather than their skill. The discipline is to name it
honestly: *"I am not diagnosing a capacity gap. You can do this; you
have shown me you can do this in our reps. The gap is willingness.
You do not want to give up the [softening / aggression / withdrawal]
that the composure would require you to give up. That is a different
kind of gap, and I cannot coach it the way I coach a capacity gap. I
can name it; you have to choose whether to work it."* The coach does
not manipulate the student into willingness; the coach names the gap
and lets the student choose. Some students will choose to work it;
some will not. The coach who is honest about the willingness gap
respects the student's agency. The coach who pretends a willingness
gap is a capacity gap, in order to keep coaching, is corrupting the
diagnosis to keep the engagement.

**The diagnosis the coach cannot make.** Sometimes the coach
recognises that the load-bearing gap is outside the coach's
competence to diagnose or to work — a deep psychological pattern, a
neurological difference, a psychiatric condition that affects
communication. The discipline is to name the limit honestly and to
refer the student to the appropriate resource, while continuing to
work the communication skills that are workable. The coach who
pretends to a competence they do not have is failing the student
and violating the moral foundation. The coach who names their limit
and refers is serving the student, even when the referral feels like
a failure. It is not a failure; it is the discipline of knowing what
the coaching can and cannot do.

**The student who has been misdiagnosed by a previous coach.** Some
students arrive having been misdiagnosed by a previous coach — told
the gap was X when the gap was Y, sometimes for years. The student
has internalised the misdiagnosis and may resist the new, correct
diagnosis precisely because accepting it means accepting that the
previous coach was wrong, which is destabilising. The discipline is
to deliver the new diagnosis with the evidence, to acknowledge the
cost of the prior misdiagnosis (without disparaging the prior coach,
who may have been acting in good faith with limited information),
and to give the student time to update their self-understanding.
The student who has been misdiagnosed for years may need several
sessions to internalise the new diagnosis, and the coach's patience
is part of the diagnostic work.

## Self-Check Questions for the Coach

Before forming any diagnosis, and after delivering one, the coach
asks:

1. *Am I diagnosing from the student's self-report, or from the
   observed rep?* If the former, run a rep before forming the
   diagnosis.

2. *Am I diagnosing from a single rep, or from a pattern across
   reps?* If from a single rep, run a second rep in a different
   scenario to confirm.

3. *Have I identified the first point of collapse, and traced
   upstream from it?* If not, the diagnosis is at the level of the
   symptom, not the cause.

4. *Have I run the leverage test on the candidate gaps?* If not, I
   may be working the most visible gap rather than the load-bearing
   one.

5. *Am I diagnosing the gap I am comfortable correcting, or the gap
   the evidence supports?* If the former, name the comfort and look
   again.

6. *Am I diagnosing the gap that flatters my preferred pole, or the
   gap that serves the student's approach to the dual benchmark?* If
   the former, the diagnosis is contaminated by preference.

7. *Have I re-checked the prior diagnosis against this session's
   fresh rep?* If the prior diagnosis has not been re-checked, it is
   stale.

8. *Does the diagnosis respect sequencing — am I diagnosing a gap at
   a level the student has the prerequisites for?* If not, the real
   gap is at the prerequisite level, and I should diagnose there.

## Worked Micro-Examples

### Example A — Diagnosing upstream of the self-report

M—— arrives at session S-025 reporting: "I need to work on my words.
In the conversation with my director last week, I couldn't find the
right words, and I ended up just agreeing to something I shouldn't
have." She has been thinking about it all week. She has a list of
"better words" she wishes she had used.

The coach receives the self-report as a clue, not a diagnosis. The
coach asks M—— to role-play the conversation with the director, with
the coach playing the director. The coach runs the rep at the same
level of pressure as the original conversation. M—— begins well; the
words are fine. The coach, as director, applies mild pressure —
*"I really need this from you, M——."* — and the coach watches.

The first point of collapse is not in the words. The words M——
reaches for are fine. The collapse is in the body: a small breath
catch, a hand movement, a quarter-second of eye contact breaking,
and then a softening of the voice that signals, before any word is
spoken, that M—— is about to concede. The concession follows the
softening, not the other way around. The words were the last thing
to go; the composure was the first.

The coach marks the first collapse: the breath catch, four seconds
before the verbal concession. The coach traces upstream: *what
capacity, had it been present, would have prevented the breath catch?*
Composure. The leverage test: *if composure were closed, would the
downstream collapses dissolve?* Yes — if M—— had held composure, the
softening would not have signalled, the concession would not have
followed, and the words she already had would have been sufficient.

The coach runs a second rep, a different scenario — a colleague
asking her to cover for them, lower stakes than the director — and
watches. The same pattern: words are fine, breath catches, voice
softens, concession follows. The pattern is structural. The
diagnosis is confirmed.

The coach delivers the diagnosis plainly: *"Your self-report was
'words.' My diagnosis is composure. The words you reached for were
fine in both reps. The first collapse was not in the words; it was
in the breath, four seconds before the verbal concession. The
concession followed the composure collapse, not the other way around.
If we close composure, the words you already have will land. If we
work words, you'll have better words delivered from the same
composure gap, which is the same problem with nicer packaging."*

M—— sits with it. She is sceptical — she has been thinking about
words all week. The coach invites her to test the diagnosis: *"Next
time you have a conversation where you feel the pull to concede,
watch your breath. If the breath catches before the words fail, the
diagnosis is composure. If the words fail first, I'm wrong and we
revisit."* M—— agrees. The diagnosis is held as a hypothesis she
can test in the field. The next session's rep will confirm or
revise it.

### Example B — The leverage test resolving two candidate gaps

D—— arrives at session S-026 reporting: "I'm losing deals. I had two
this month that I should have closed, and they fell apart at the end.
I think I'm being too aggressive. I need to soften." D—— is a senior
engineer who has moved into a client-facing role; the warmth gap
(named in `assets/example-feedback.md`) has been closing slowly but
the Shelby pole is still his default under pressure.

The coach runs a rep: D—— selling a contract to a counterpart (the
coach playing the counterpart). The rep is good — D—— holds frame,
the warmth is present in the opening, the technical case is strong.
The collapse comes near the close: the counterpart raises a concern
about timeline, D—— pushes back firmly but with a slight edge, the
counterpart visibly stiffens, and the conversation ends without
commitment.

Two candidate gaps are visible. The first is the gap D—— reported:
aggression at the close. The second is a warmth gap that surfaced
specifically at the moment of the counterpart's concern — D——'s
tone shifted from warm (opening) to precise (technical case) to
sharp (close), and the sharpness was the counterpart's exit cue.

The coach applies the leverage test. If the aggression gap is
load-bearing, closing it would require D—— to soften the close. But
the coach notices: the sharpness was not a failure of softness
technique; it was a *reaction* to the counterpart's concern, and
the reaction was a self-protective move against the possibility of
losing the deal. The sharpness was downstream of an underlying
frame instability — D—— could not hold the frame of "this deal may
not close, and that is acceptable," and the inability to hold that
frame produced the sharpness that closed the deal prematurely. The
aggression was the symptom; the frame instability under the threat
of loss was the cause.

If the frame gap is closed, does the aggression dissolve? Yes — D——
would be able to hold the counterpart's concern without sharpness,
because the concern would not threaten the frame. If the aggression
gap is closed without the frame gap being closed, does the deal
close? Probably not — D—— would soften the close but the underlying
instability would surface elsewhere (over-accommodation, premature
concession, or a different form of self-protection). The leverage
test points to frame.

The coach delivers the diagnosis: *"Your self-report was
'aggression.' My diagnosis is frame instability under the threat of
loss. The sharpness at the close was not a softness problem; it was
a frame problem. You could not hold the frame that the deal might
not close, and the instability produced the sharpness that closed
it for you. If we close the frame gap, the sharpness will dissolve
on its own — you'll be able to hold the concern without defending
against it. If we soften the sharpness without closing the frame,
you'll find a new self-protective move, because the frame
instability will still be there."*

D—— resists. He came to work softness; the coach is prescribing
frame. The coach acknowledges the resistance and invites the test:
*"In your next deal, watch what happens at the moment of the
counterpart's concern. If you can hold the frame that the deal may
not close, does the sharpness still surface? If yes, I'm wrong, and
softness is the gap. If no, frame is the gap."* The diagnosis is
held as a hypothesis, testable in the field, revisable in the next
session. The leverage test pointed to the gap that, if closed,
would unblock the most downstream gaps; the diagnosis is offered
for the student's testing, not imposed as a verdict.

---

*Diagnosis is the act of seeing the student clearly enough to know
what is actually in the way. It is harder than it looks, because the
student's self-report, the visible symptom, and the coach's own
comfortable categories all conspire to point at the wrong gap. Train
the discipline: take a real rep, find the first collapse, trace
upstream, run the leverage test, confirm with a second rep, name the
diagnosis plainly, re-check it every session. The student who is
correctly diagnosed is half-coached; the student who is misdiagnosed
is fully blocked, no matter how good the intervention would have
been for the wrong gap. Get the diagnosis right, and the rest of the
coaching has somewhere to land.*
